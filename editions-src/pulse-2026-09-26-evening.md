## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Ethiopia's north went dark in a night. East Africa's exposure is the map, not the airport.**

The taxi driver was waiting for a fare at Mekelle's airport when the fighters arrived. They carried light weapons, he told the Associated Press, and ordered him to leave because no flights would land. By the afternoon of 23 September Ethiopian Airlines, the only carrier flying scheduled services into Tigray, had suspended Mekelle, Axum and Shire. "We will inform you once flight operations resume," the airline said.

Those are short domestic hops. They are not the aircraft that carry a German couple to Kilimanjaro. But the airports were a symptom, and the condition behind them reaches East Africa's order books by routes most operators have not mapped.

It took less than a week. On 20 September the Tigray People's Liberation Front announced an alliance with six other armed groups aimed at overthrowing Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. On the Wednesday the airports went. Getachew Reda, an adviser to the prime minister, wrote that the front had "taken over federally controlled airports in Tigray," adding that its leadership had "taken irresponsibility to new heights." Tigray's government said it was answering "intensive bombardments" and had "entered into a defensive battle."

By Friday Washington had said the sentence that matters to anyone selling travel. "U.S. citizens are advised not to travel to the Amhara Region (including Lalibela), the Tigray Region, or the Afar Region for any reason," the embassy in Addis Ababa said. London had widened its no-go zone in Afar a day earlier, on top of standing advice against all travel to Tigray and Amhara.

The African Union's chairperson, Mahmoud Ali Youssouf, warned that seizing the civilian airports risked "eroding the hard-won gains" of the 2022 Pretoria Agreement. Alan Boswell of the International Crisis Group was blunter. "So this is all becoming one big regional mess," he told Al Jazeera, "and it could get very, very ugly if we don't see de-escalation."

The instinctive East African question is the hub. It has the most reassuring answer, for now. Nothing issued this week touches Addis Ababa or Bole. Britain's map carves out the corridors around the capital, down to the ten kilometres either side of the road to Bishoftu. The suspensions cover northern domestic points only.

That is the misread. The exposure does not run through Bole's runway. It runs through three other channels.

The first is the itinerary. Lalibela's churches and Axum's stelae are World Heritage sites, and both now sit behind the warnings. For British travellers the region was already closed on paper; what changed is that the aircraft stopped and Washington said "for any reason". Wherever an agent has paired Lalibela with a safari further south, that leg is now unsellable for the season, and its nights need a home.

Here is the part nobody in our trade has said aloud. The obvious substitutes are on the Swahili coast: Stone Town and Lamu Old Town are World Heritage sites too, a short flight from the safari circuit. And on 25 September, by coincidence, France redrew its Kenya map and moved Lamu and Manda islands from orange to yellow, air access only. Paris did not move because of Ethiopia. But an agent re-routing a displaced week will find Lamu cleaner on the French map than at any point since at least March.

The second channel is the buyer's map, and it is less comfortable. Advisories bleed across borders in the mind of whoever signs off a trip. Kenya's frontier with Ethiopia already sits inside every major no-go zone. A duty-of-care officer now sees "Ethiopia" and "fighting" in the same month that France keeps a pre-electoral warning on Kenya, and the same week the US Embassy in Nairobi warned that Ebola-screening data collected at Kenya's ports of entry may have been breached. None of it moved Kenya's advisories: British, American, Canadian and German advice was unchanged when we read it on 26 September. Your sales team should be able to say so in one dated sentence before a client asks.

The third channel is time. After the last war, Ethiopian resumed Mekelle on 28 December 2022 and daily Axum flights only in June 2024. A new war in the north will be measured in seasons, not weeks.

So: city hotels in Nairobi, Kampala and Kigali should brief reservations tonight. Coast properties in Lamu and Zanzibar should get in front of the specialists who sell Ethiopia's north, with heritage nights priced for the displaced week. Camps with guests routing through Addis should watch three things: any British or American language on Addis or Bole, any Ethiopian notice beyond the northern domestic points, and whether the AU's mediation produces talks.

The war in Ethiopia's north will not close East Africa's front door. It will test whether our hotels can tell buyers, precisely and quickly, which doors are still open.
