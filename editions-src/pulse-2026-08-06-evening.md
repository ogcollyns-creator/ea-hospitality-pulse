## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**What Washington's $242 million really tells East African operators**

On 5 August, the US State Department published a short Media Note most East African hoteliers will never read. They should. In two paragraphs it did two things that matter to anyone selling a Ugandan lodge, a Nairobi conference hotel or a Zanzibar beach resort into the American market.

First, it announced a further $242 million for Ebola response and preparedness in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda, taking the State Department's direct assistance past $512 million — part of a US commitment of up to $500 million made at the G7 in June. Second, in the same breath, it confirmed that the Travel Advisories for the DRC, Uganda and South Sudan "remain at Level 4 – Do Not Travel," while commending Uganda for "swift and decisive action."

Hold those two facts side by side, because the gap between them is the whole story.

Uganda declared its own outbreak over on 28 July. Kampala's last confirmed case was in June; it has begun the 42-day countdown that, if it holds, ends around 27 August. The epidemiology inside Uganda is genuinely reassuring. And yet the US advisory has not moved, and Washington is still funding border screening and preparedness as if the risk window is wide open.

That is not a contradiction. It is how travel-risk policy actually works. The Level 4 on Uganda was never really about Uganda's own case count — it was about the fire next door. The DRC outbreak is now the largest in that country's history, surpassing the 2018–2020 epidemic: 3,605 confirmed cases and 1,587 deaths as at 30 July, per WHO figures reported by UN News on 3 August, with the most recent weekly data climbing past 3,800 and a record 567 cases in a single week. As long as cross-border transmission is accelerating, the US will keep Uganda inside the regional cordon — commendation or not.

For operators, that commendation is the tell worth reading. It signals goodwill toward Kampala is building, and that when the epidemiological case for a downgrade finally arrives — most plausibly after Uganda's 42-day all-clear — the political resistance to acting on it will be low. The direction of travel is positive. The timing is not yet.

So here is the operator translation. If your Q3 and early-Q4 revenue plan assumes a September rebound in US-source demand off the back of an advisory easing, take that assumption out. The realistic sequence is: the US CDC renews its entry suspension on or around 12 August with the DRC — and Uganda — still listed; Uganda completes its countdown around 27 August; and only then does a downgrade become a live possibility, with a further lag before it feeds bookings. A US-market recovery is a late-Q4 story at the earliest, not a September one.

None of this is a reason to discount. This is where properties get a risk window wrong: they cut rate defensively against a demand shock that, for most of the region, has not arrived. Kenya remains Level 2. Tanzania, Zanzibar and Rwanda sit at Level 3, unchanged. The board has not moved against your property this week. Your European, regional and domestic source markets are reading the DRC headlines, not cancelling Serengeti or Diani. Holding rate through the shoulder and into the September–October peak is the defensible call; discounting on someone else's outbreak is not.

Watch three dates: 12 August, when the CDC renewal decision falls; 18 August, when the WHO IHR Emergency Committee reconvenes on the PHEIC; and around 27 August, Uganda's 42-day all-clear. The first two will confirm the status quo. The third is the one that could finally start moving Uganda's number — and the $242 million announced this week is Washington quietly buying the confidence to eventually do so.
