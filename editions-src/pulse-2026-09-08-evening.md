## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**East Africa no longer has one cost base**

In the year to July 2026, the price of restaurant and accommodation services rose 14.2% in Rwanda and 2.7% in Tanzania. Same month, same statistical category, neighbouring markets, a fivefold gap. For a region that markets itself as a single circuit and is underwritten by lenders as a single risk, that is a larger fact than it looks.

The Rwandan numbers come from the National Institute of Statistics of Rwanda, released on 10 August. Headline inflation reached 14.5%. Transport rose 24.2%, housing, water, electricity, gas and other fuels 21.0%, food 13.1%. The National Bank of Rwanda's Monetary Policy Committee, meeting on 26 August, put second-quarter core inflation at 12.3% and energy inflation at 45.7%. The following day it raised the policy rate to 8.75%, a third consecutive increase and, per Bloomberg, the highest in seventeen years. BNR expects inflation to average 13.1% this year, ease to around 7.9% next, and return to its 2–8% target band only in the second half of 2027.

Most of the commentary read this as a household story. It is also a currency story, and that is the part being missed.

Rwandan operators earning dollars have long been carried by the franc. Governor John Rwangombwa told Parliament in November 2024 that it had depreciated 16.3% over the 2023/24 fiscal year, a record. In a year like that, devaluation quietly pays for local cost inflation: the same dollar rate card buys more local input every month, and the pressure never reaches the rate conversation.

That mechanism has switched off. BNR reports the franc depreciated 0.5% in the first quarter of 2026, supported by exports up 63% and reserves above four months of import cover. Currency stability is, in most respects, exactly what the country has been working towards.

But it inverts the arithmetic for anyone selling in dollars. A rate card held flat year-on-year now buys roughly 14% less of the Rwandan cost base than it did in July 2025. Nothing on the rate sheet changed. The margin moved anyway.

The second-order point is that this is a contract-duration problem, not a quarterly one.

Rwanda's tourism model is unusually long-dated. RDB's 2025 annual report, published on 28 April, puts MICE revenue at US$94.7 million from 165 international and regional events, up from US$84.8 million. Congress business is won on bids placed years in advance and priced in dollars at the time of the bid. Visit Rwanda's own commitments run the same way, with sponsorships extending to 2028 and 2030. Gorilla-trekking product enters operator brochures twelve to eighteen months ahead.

Compound BNR's own published forecast — 13.1% this year, roughly 7.9% next — and you get about 22% of cumulative cost inflation sitting inside prices that in many cases are already agreed. The exposure is largest in precisely the segment Rwanda has been best at.

Three practical readings.

For Kigali city and MICE properties: audit every rate fixed beyond March 2027 — congress, group, corporate agreements, DMC allocations. If you are bidding for 2028, price off the central bank's forecast path rather than today's cost base, and put an energy-escalation clause into anything signed this quarter.

For bush properties around Volcanoes, Nyungwe and Akagera: your exposure is transport, up 24.2%, and energy, up 21%. It is not the permit, which is a dollar pass-through. Brochure rates for 2027 go out in the next few weeks. Set them against those two lines, not against a headline number.

For beach and regional programmes out of Zanzibar and Diani: this is a competitive advantage, and a temporary one. On 2027 multi-country programmes the Tanzanian and Kenyan legs will hold value better than the Rwandan leg. Say so to your DMCs now. The window closes when BNR's tightening works, which BNR itself expects in the second half of 2027.

The regional habit is to treat East Africa as one cost story carrying one currency risk. For most of the past decade that was roughly true. This year it is not.
