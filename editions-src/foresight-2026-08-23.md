## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**East Africa Is Fencing the Tourist Shilling — Just as the Growth Guest Becomes a Neighbour**

In August 2026, four East African governments made four separate moves to control who collects tourism money and who is legally permitted to earn it. Zanzibar arrested three East African nationals for running excursions reserved for Zanzibaris. Kenya proposed rewriting who collects and manages its 2% hotel levy. Tanzania handed its parks back 51% of gate revenue. Rwanda continues to price access to a gorilla by passport. Read separately, these are governance stories. Read together, they mark a change in the unit of competition — from attracting visitors to fencing their spend inside a national perimeter.

If you own a lodge in the Serengeti, a resort on Nungwi or a city hotel in Nairobi, this is a live question about which lines of your P&L you will still be allowed to earn in three years.

**THE SITUATION**

On 18 August, the Zanzibar Commission for Tourism and the Immigration Department arrested three people for conducting tour and excursion operations. One was Kenyan, two were Ugandan. Under Zanzibar's Tourism Act 2009, tour operating and shuttle-boat businesses are reserved for Zanzibaris, which makes citizens of neighbouring EAC states foreigners at the excursion desk — a position the reporting itself flags as sitting awkwardly against the EAC Common Market Protocol on free movement of labour and services (The Citizen / Daily Nation, 21–22 August 2026).

In Nairobi the same week, the Ministry of Tourism and Wildlife told the Departmental Committee it wants the Tourism Fund to keep collecting the 2% levy on regulated hotels and restaurants while the Cabinet Secretary oversees management of the proceeds. The Tourism (Amendment) Bill 2026 would also widen the Fund's mandate into product development, events and destination marketing (Capital FM / allAfrica, 21 August 2026). It landed alongside MPs' resistance to absorbing the Tourism Research Institute into the Kenya Tourism Board.

In Tanzania, TANAPA and the Ngorongoro Conservation Area Authority regain the right to retain 51% of the revenue they generate, held in special Bank of Tanzania accounts and released with Paymaster General approval — reversing the 2020 arrangement that swept collections to the central fund (Minister Pindi Chana, May 2026).

Rwanda already runs the most explicit version: a gorilla permit at US$1,500 for international visitors, roughly US$200 for Rwandan and EAC citizens on production of national identification, and US$1,275 for MICE delegates staying three nights or more (RDB tariff, 2026), with 10% of park revenue shared with neighbouring communities.

**WHAT EVERYONE IS MISSING**

Not one of these measures generates an additional visitor. Every one redistributes money that already exists. The industry has read them as separate items of governance news when they are, collectively, a fiscal redesign — and the perimeter is being drawn at the national border, not the regional one.

That timing is the problem. The fastest-growing guest in East Africa is now regional. Kenyan residents accounted for 45% of hotel occupancy in 2025, and hotel bed-nights rose 12.6% to 11.56 million (KNBS Economic Survey, April 2026). Uganda alone supplied 234,556 arrivals to Kenya in 2025. The region is hardening a national-perimeter architecture at precisely the moment its marginal booking comes from someone who must cross one of those perimeters to reach you.

The second-order effect is where owners should be looking. Growth in most East African P&Ls is migrating out of room revenue — levy-assessed, hard-currency-priced, wholesaler-fed — and into the experience layer: permits, gate fees, transfers, excursions, guiding. That layer is exactly what is being nationalised. Where your incremental margin now lives, the state is no longer merely your tax collector. It is your licensor, increasingly your price-setter, and in Zanzibar's case the guarantor of a competitor's exclusive right to sell what you were selling.

**WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT**

*Beach and island operators:* run a nationality audit on your ancillary chain this week — not just your own licence, but the licence holder behind every excursion, transfer and boat product you sell or subcontract. Enforcement has moved from paperwork to arrests.

*Bush and safari operators:* a park authority funding its own roads and gates has both the means and the motive to reprice them. Model a Serengeti and Ngorongoro fee increase into 2027/28 costings, and put fee-escalation language into contracts you sign this quarter.

*City hotels in Kenya:* the Amendment Bill is in public participation. A submission costs an afternoon and decides whether your levy funds marketing you benefit from or institutions you do not.

*Everyone:* stop pricing East Africa as one market, and stop pricing it as five. Price it as a set of residency tiers, because that is what your governments have built. Publish a regional rate that assumes a shorter, later, local-currency booking, and route it direct rather than through a distribution stack designed for a European wholesaler.

**THE WATCH**

Whether Zanzibar's 18 August action is an isolated case or the opening of a campaign. Whether the EAC Secretariat says anything about a member state treating other members' citizens as foreign labour. And whether Kenya's Budget Review and Outlook Paper, due at Cabinet around 26 August, carries a named tourism charge — the instinct that fences the excursion counter also reaches for the levy.
