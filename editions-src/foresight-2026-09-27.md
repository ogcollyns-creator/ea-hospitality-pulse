## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Tanzania built a $44 travel-insurance mandate that survives contact with a border post. Kenya built a $50,000 one that hasn't survived contact with a judge. The lesson for every entry rule East Africa writes next isn't in either headline — it's in the gap between them.**

If you sell a Kenya-into-Zanzibar itinerary, you are about to hand your guest a compliance step nobody warned them about. From 1 October, foreign visitors entering mainland Tanzania or Zanzibar without a Tanzanian-issued travel insurance policy — $44, sold through the National Insurance Corporation or, on the island, the Zanzibar Insurance Corporation — can be refused entry outright. The instrument is Government Notice No. 256 of 2026, gazetted 4 September. It covers medical evacuation, repatriation and lost luggage for up to 92 days, exempts EAC and SADC nationals, residents and transit passengers, and can be bought online or at the border. It has drawn coverage, questions and some grumbling from tour operators about one more line item — but no legal challenge, and no sign of one.

**THE SITUATION**

Compare that with what Kenya has been through since it tried the same idea in reverse order of care. In August, Health Cabinet Secretary Aden Duale gazetted a mandatory travel-health insurance requirement pricing cover at roughly Sh6.4 million — about $50,000 — per foreign visitor. On 24 August, Justice Francis Rayola Olel granted interim orders suspending it, after petitioners argued the Health Ministry had no authority to impose entry conditions that belong under the Citizenship and Immigration Act, and after the Consumers Federation of Kenya separately challenged the lack of public participation and the unclear process for selecting the insurers who would underwrite it. A full hearing was set for 16 September. As of this week, no ruling from that hearing has surfaced in any source we can find — the mandate remains suspended, unresolved, five weeks and counting.

Both governments wanted the same outcome: a financial backstop so a foreign visitor's medical emergency doesn't become a hospital's unpaid bill or the state's problem. Tanzania built a product a tourist could plausibly buy and an insurer could plausibly underwrite, routed it through the regulator that already licenses travel insurance, and priced it in the range of the departure taxes and park fees travellers already budget for. Kenya built a number closer to a bond than a premium, imposed it by a health directive that bypassed the immigration authority actually responsible for entry conditions, and never resolved who the approved insurers were before petitioners forced the question in court.

**WHAT EVERYONE IS MISSING**

The trade press has filed these as two separate national stories — "Tanzania launches," "Kenya blocked" — because most desks cover one country at a time. Read together, they say something about where the friction actually lands: not evenly across East Africa, but unevenly across a single trip.

The dominant premium itinerary in this region is not single-country. It is Kenya safari into Zanzibar beach, or Kenya-Tanzania combined circuits running Mara into Serengeti. From 1 October, that guest clears immigration in Nairobi with no insurance gate at all — Kenya's own attempt is frozen — and then hits a hard, unfamiliar $44-per-person purchase requirement, proof demanded on arrival, the moment the same trip crosses into Tanzania. Uganda and Rwanda, bound to Kenya inside the East Africa Tourist Visa, carry no equivalent requirement and have signalled no plan to introduce one. Tanzania sits outside that visa union by design and can move on its own timetable — which is precisely the freedom it just used. The story is not East Africa adopting a global norm; plenty of destinations already require proof of cover, so the instrument itself is unremarkable. The story is a five-country bloc landing on the same idea at different speeds, with the fastest mover sitting outside the bloc's own visa coordination — and the asymmetry showing up mid-itinerary, where no single desk is watching for it.

**WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT**

*Beach — Zanzibar and the coast:* put the $44 requirement into every pre-arrival communication this week, not next month. A guest arriving at Abeid Amani Karume without having bought or been told about the policy is a delay at best and a refused entry at worst, four days from now.

*Bush — cross-border safari operators:* if your itinerary runs a Mara-to-Serengeti or similar Kenya-Tanzania transfer, build proof-of-purchase into the pre-departure checklist alongside passport and yellow-fever documentation. A guest turned back at a remote border post is now an operational failure, not a footnote.

*City — Nairobi, Kampala, Kigali:* watch Kenya's litigation rather than assuming the idea has died with the injunction. The government's underlying intent — a financial backstop against foreign-visitor medical costs — hasn't been abandoned, only its first instrument. If Kenya returns with a Tanzania-style product routed through the Insurance Regulatory Authority and priced sensibly, it will likely arrive with less warning than the first attempt, precisely because the government now knows a Gazette Notice from Health alone doesn't survive review. Draft the guest-communication template now, before you need it on short notice.

**THE WATCH**

Kenya's High Court ruling following the 16 September hearing — still unreported as this goes to press. Tanzania's first fortnight of enforcement from 1 October, and whether any refused-entry incidents surface at JRO, ZNZ or DAR. Whether Uganda or Rwanda float their own version once Tanzania's rollout is seen to work.

Two governments reached for the same tool this year. One built something an insurer, a court and a tourist could all live with. The other is still finding out what happens when you skip that part.
