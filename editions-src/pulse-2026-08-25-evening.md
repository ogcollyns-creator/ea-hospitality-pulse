A Mara camp discounting to fill climbs a steeper cliff than a roadside hotel in Ohio.

## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Your most exposed property is not your cheapest one. It is your most expensive undifferentiated one.**

American hotel data published this morning contains the clearest lesson on discounting anyone in East Africa will read this year. Almost nobody here will read it, because it is filed under "US market".

**THE SITUATION**

The H1 2026 Hotel Profitability Report from HotelData.com, written up by Sarah McCay Tams of Actabl on Hospitality Net on 25 August 2026, splits the American market by chain scale.

Luxury hotels raised ADR 10.1%, from USD 302.97 to USD 333.49, *and* occupancy 3.4 points, to 67.5%. RevPAR rose 15.9%; total revenue per available room rose 15.1%, so the money kept flowing after the room was sold. In the second quarter the pattern intensified: ADR up 12.6%, RevPAR up 18.4%.

Economy hotels did not lose their guests. They gained the most demand of any chain scale in the sample — occupancy up 4.6 points, to 68.5%. But ADR fell 9.3%, from USD 120.34 to USD 109.11, and RevPAR ended the half down 2.7%.

STR and Tourism Economics, in their August 2026 outlook, put a number on why: American households earning USD 200,000 or more are about 11% of households and roughly 25% of travel spending.

**WHAT EVERYONE IS MISSING**

The story is being told as rich travellers versus squeezed ones. That reading is comfortable and wrong, because it points to a conclusion — go upmarket — that most operators cannot act on and some should not.

Look again at the economy segment. Its problem was not demand; occupancy rose more there than anywhere else in the market. The discounting worked exactly as intended. It filled the house and destroyed revenue anyway, because a sold room is not a free room: housekeeping, water, power, distribution and labour all rise with volume.

That is the part that transfers to East Africa, and with more force than it carries in America. The marginal cost of an occupied bed-night in a bush camp — diesel for game drives and generators, trucked water, staff-to-guest ratios, supply runs on bad roads — is a multiple of the cost of turning a room in a US limited-service hotel. Nairobi diesel sits at KSh 217.86 a litre this cycle, roughly KSh 14.28 above the KSh 203.58 landed costs implied, per EPRA's 14 August notice reported by Business Daily. A Mara camp that discounts to fill climbs a steeper cliff than a roadside hotel in Ohio, not a gentler one.

So the dividing line is not price point. It is substitutability. A guest who can find the same product 400 metres away will make you compete on rate; a guest who cannot, will not. By that test, East Africa's most exposed properties are not its cheapest. They are the four-star business boxes in Westlands, Kololo and Kigali that differ only by carpet, and the near-identical premium tented camps sold at premium rates inside the same conservancy. High price is not pricing power. Pricing power is the absence of a substitute.

Kenya is already living the consequence. The Tourism Research Institute's own 2025 annual report puts earnings per arrival at KSh 189,004, up 3.13% on 2024, against the report's own inflation assumption of 4.075%. Real revenue per visitor fell about 0.9% — the economy-segment pattern, written across an entire country.

**WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT**

City hotels: write down what a guest cannot get from the property next door. If the list is empty, stop bidding corporate rate and start building the thing that fills it. A rate war between identical boxes has one winner, and it is the buyer.

Bush lodges and camps: calculate cost per occupied bed-night with diesel and water in it, set a rate floor, and refuse to cross it. Agents will push; the floor is the answer. Separately, EPRA's own landed-cost figure kills any supplier fuel surcharge this cycle — use it.

Beach resorts: measure TRevPAR, not RevPAR. American luxury's total revenue per available room grew 15.1% because the guest kept spending after check-in. In Diani and Zanzibar, where supply keeps arriving and the product is easy to copy, spend beyond the room is the defensible margin.

Everyone: never let an occupancy number reach your board without the rate and the variable cost beside it. A full house is a claim, not a result.

**THE WATCH**

EPRA's next fuel review, around 14 September — with Kenya's 8% petroleum VAT rate expiring 14 October. KNBS Leading Economic Indicators, monthly, for arrivals against hotel bed-nights. And STR's September US outlook, which forecasts double-digit luxury RevPAR growth in Q3 and 5.9% full-year luxury ADR growth. If that holds, top-end price tolerance is structural rather than a post-pandemic echo.

Occupancy is the easiest number to move and the easiest one to be fooled by. Ask what you sold, not how many.
