## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Kenya spent Thursday arguing about the price. On Friday it appointed the people who will spend the money.**

The notice ran to a few lines and named three people, which is roughly why nobody read it.

On 18 September 2026, under the Tourism Act, Cap. 381, Kenya's Cabinet Secretary for Tourism and Wildlife gazetted a reconstitution of the Tourism Fund Board of Trustees. Patrick Ngere was re-appointed for three years; Nicholas Kimeli and Dr Yvonne Tharao were appointed for three-year terms of their own. In the same issue, Rose Polyne Nyambura was re-appointed to the Council of Kenya Utalii College and Dr Dorothy Jepkoech Rotich appointed beside her. Two seats on the Kenya Wildlife Service Board of Trustees were renewed for a further three years. Every appointment took effect on the day of publication.

Board appointments are the dullest paper the state produces, and this one appears to have gone unreported in the trade. That is a pity, given what had happened in Nairobi the previous morning.

On Thursday 17 September, nine trade associations filed into a Senate committee room to argue about money: the hotelkeepers, the tour operators, the travel agents, the coast association and the air operators among them. Their complaint concerned Legal Notice No. 127 of 2026, the Tourism (Tourism Enterprises) Regulations, and a fee schedule none of them had been shown before it was gazetted.

Senator Mwenda Gataya of Tharaka Nithi, who chairs the Committee on Delegated Legislation, gave them the assurance the occasion required. "We have heard your concerns and the Committee will carefully interrogate every issue raised," he said. "We will engage the regulator and consider all the submissions before making an informed decision on whether to annul or accede to the Regulations."

That decision has not been made. The instrument is live and undecided. On the following morning, the state seated for three years the trustees of the fund that collects the industry's money.

There is nothing improper in this. Board terms expire and are renewed, and a notice landing the day after a hearing is a coincidence of the parliamentary calendar. But sequence is not conspiracy, and this sequence shows where decisions are actually taken.

The Tourism Fund collects two per cent of revenue from regulated hotels and restaurants, and disburses it to the Kenya Tourism Board, the Tourism Research Institute, Bomas of Kenya and Kenya Utalii College. It is a levy on your top line, administered by a board, spent on bodies whose councils the same Cabinet Secretary appoints. On 18 September, two of those three sets of appointments were made in one gazette.

Now set the two numbers side by side, because the industry has spent a month looking at the smaller one. A licence fee is a fixed annual sum, paid once regardless of how the year goes. The levy is a percentage of everything you sell. For any property with meaningful turnover the second is larger by a wide margin, and unlike the first it scales with your success. An operator who wins the argument about the licence fee and loses the argument about the levy has won the wrong argument.

Which brings us to the drafting that has drawn almost no attention. In August the Ministry put amendments to the Tourism Act before the National Assembly's Departmental Committee on Tourism and Wildlife, chaired by the Maara MP Kareke Mbiuki. The headline was procedural: separate collection from management. Read the detail and the Cabinet Secretary's expanded responsibilities include, in the reported language of the proposal, "determining the amounts of money payable for the Fund and formulating the conditions for disbursement."

Julius Bitok, the Principal Secretary, defended the architecture by analogy. "We are borrowing the approach underpinned by the Public Finance Management Act where KRA collects money through e-Citizen and deposits the funds with CBK for allocation through the Treasury," he said.

It is a reasonable analogy, and it is worth noticing how far it reaches. KRA does collect and remit on that model. But KRA collects at rates Parliament sets in statute, and cannot change them because a ministry finds the yield inconvenient. The analogy describes the plumbing. A clause about determining amounts payable is not plumbing. It is the tap.

At the same hearing Mbiuki challenged the Ministry to widen the levy base beyond hospitality to take in Airbnb. The same package proposes merging the Kenya Tourism Board with the Tourism Research Institute. So the base is widening, the rate-setting question is in play, two receiving institutions may be merged, and the trustees who sit over the fund have three-year mandates. None of it is secret; each piece was reported once, somewhere. What nobody has done is put them on one page, where they read differently.

Three years from 18 September 2026 runs to September 2029. Kenya's general election falls on 10 August 2027. These appointments outlast this Parliament, the election, and the whole 2027 contracting season operators are pricing now.

So, practically: stop treating the levy as a fixed cost. City hotels have modelled two per cent as a constant because it has been one; if the rate moves from statute into policy it becomes a variable belonging in a sensitivity analysis, and anyone signing 2027 group business should carry a statutory-cost-variation clause. Note too who is most exposed, which is not who you would assume. Bush and beach properties running seasonal, high-rate, low-volume models carry a levy bill that swings hard with a good season, where a fixed licence fee is indifferent.

And note which room is still open. "We are inviting all stakeholders and the public to present their views on the Bill," Mbiuki said. Nine associations mustered over a schedule of fixed fees. The Bill is the larger number and it is still in committee.

One caution. The gazette notice is confirmed: those names, those bodies, that date. The Bill's status is confirmed as at the August session, and we have not established what has happened since. The reading that joins the two is ours, offered as an inference.

But it is the inference the calendar invites. On Thursday the industry was heard. On Friday the appointments were made. The question worth asking in Nairobi this week is not whether the operators argued well, because by all accounts they did. It is whether they were arguing in the room where the decision gets taken.
