## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**AFCON 2027 will be priced, not filled.**

The praise came first. At State House Entebbe on Tuesday, Bianca Wambui, the Confederation of African Football's country infrastructure manager, told President Yoweri Museveni what her inspectors had found at Uganda's stadiums and training grounds. "The progress is commendable," she said.

Then came the part that matters to anyone who sells a bed. The delegation, led by CAF secretary general Samson Adamu, had found accommodation outside the capital lagging, and the three co-hosts moving too slowly on a single visa. Museveni did not dispute it. "The Kampala area has the facilities, but now the challenge is upcountry, which we are now going to concentrate on," he said. "I will unlock whatever needs to be unlocked."

AFCON kicks off on 19 June 2027 across Uganda, Kenya and Tanzania. The instinct across the region is to count rooms and build more of them. The evidence published the same Tuesday from the biggest World Cup ever staged suggests that is the wrong exercise for most East African hotels, and a costly one for some.

Houston hosted seven matches this summer and an estimated half a million out-of-town visitors. By the end of June, according to CoStar data analysed by Bunmi Oyinloye of HVS, occupancy stood at 61.6 per cent, essentially flat on the year. Rate rose 5.8 per cent, with the money made on particular nights: roughly 52 to 53 per cent up on the eve of key matches. "When you have Super Bowl or Final Four, usually the day after they announce, you have no rooms to sell," said Emre Ozsut, regional director of sales at The Lancaster Hotel. "In this, we were not even close to that."

Houston was not an outlier. Thomas Hazinski of HVS compared all eleven American host cities against what each would have done without the tournament. Rate rose in every one, adding an estimated $680mn in rooms revenue. But occupancy fell below that baseline in seven of the eleven. Dynamic pricing, he wrote, "generated significant rate gains but also destroyed a meaningful share of the demand the market would otherwise have hosted." Ordinary travellers were priced out. Conference planners moved their meetings to other weeks or other cities.

Three of his findings travel to East Africa.

First, how much rate an event can extract depends on how full a market already is. New York, normally near 89.4 per cent in those weeks, captured the largest rate gain. Houston, at 61.2 per cent, captured one of the smallest. A full market pushes extra demand into price; a market with slack absorbs it and gains little.

Second, location. Atlanta, whose stadium sits beside its convention centre, recorded the smallest gain of all, because the crowds displaced the conference business that normally fills those hotels.

Third, who travels. Teams from countries with less disposable income, Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire and Cabo Verde among them, generated comparatively few room nights. Most AFCON supporters can be expected to come from within Africa, and shorter distances help. But the constraint is the same: people travel when they can afford it and when the border lets them. That is why Adamu spent part of Tuesday pressing for a visa that does not yet exist.

Put that against East Africa's calendar and AFCON 2027 becomes two tournaments.

In Nairobi, Kampala and Dar es Salaam the question is not whether there are rooms. It is what hotels do with rate in the weeks when the safari high season opens and the city hotels fill with guests bound for the Mara, the Serengeti and Bwindi. Those guests, and the conference groups, book early. Tournament visitors, on the American evidence, book late: the American Hotel and Lodging Association pointed in July to "shorter booking windows for events of this caliber." A hotel that holds rooms back for AFCON, or quotes tournament rates for the whole month, risks turning away its most reliable business now to wait for customers who may not decide until spring. European operators were already discussing AFCON itineraries in Paris on 16 September. They have alternatives Houston's visitors did not.

Hoima is the reverse. There the shortage is real. A CAF inspection report cited by the Daily Monitor called for at least five five-star hotels of 50 rooms or more. Seven hotels in Hoima and three in Masindi have been selected, and government has set aside Shs101.1bn in cheap credit for upgrades. But rooms built for a four-week peak earn their keep in the forty-eight weeks after it. "Build for the tournament, but design for the aftermath," the Kawowo columnist John Philip Mugabi wrote on Tuesday. The oil and gas economy may supply Hoima's year-round demand. The case for each new room has to close on 2028, not on June 2027.

For owners the conclusions are unglamorous. Look up your own June and July occupancy for the last two years before deciding anything. Protect contracted safari, conference and group business first, then price the match nights and the nights before them, not the month. Put an event clause in 2027 group contracts now. Bush and beach properties should sell add-on nights to travelling supporters rather than reprice. Anyone building upcountry should underwrite on the year after the tournament.

A tournament does not fill a city. It tells the city's hotels what they can charge, and it punishes those that forget who was already sleeping there.
