## LINKEDIN BIG READ

The most quoted number in Kenyan hospitality this month is the one that has not moved. Petrol held at KSh 214.03 a litre and diesel at KSh 222.86 through the 15 July–14 August cycle, and most operators filed it under stability. That reading is the mistake. The price is flat not because the market settled, but because the state is holding it down — and it is holding it down with instruments that have expiry dates.

Two props are doing the work. The first is roughly KSh 945 million of Petroleum Development Levy money applied to cushion this cycle. The second, and the one to diarise, is the 8% VAT rate on petroleum products — half the statutory 16% — which has been extended only to 14 October 2026. Both are discretionary policy choices, not market conditions. When the VAT concession reverts, fuel does not drift up on global crude; it steps up at source, by policy, on a known date.

That would matter anywhere. It matters more in Kenya because of where the country already sits on cost. Headline inflation is 6.4% (KNBS, June 2026), but the headline flatters the picture: core inflation is just 3.1%, while non-core runs at 15.1%. Nearly all the pressure is in energy, transport and food — precisely the lines a hotel carries in its kitchen, its laundry, its generators and its transfer fleet. Headline relief has not been margin relief, and the props are the only reason the energy line is not worse. There is a second, quieter cost already moving underneath the fuel price: Kenya's electricity pass-through charges — the monthly fuel-energy-cost and forex adjustments that sit below the headline tariff — now add more than KSh 5 to every unit and rose again in July. For a property running pumps, cold rooms and air-conditioning, that is a five-figure monthly swing that never appears in a rate negotiation.

Now look across the border. Tanzania's inflation is 4.0% and easing, with food pressure falling (NBS, June 2026); it remains the region's most benign cost base. Today the delivered-cost gap between operating in Nairobi and operating in Arusha or on the Zanzibar coast is close to a rounding error. That is the part the market is mispricing. The gap is not static — it is scheduled to widen, because one side of it is held up by a subsidy with a 14 October demolition date and the other is not. An operator pricing 2027 contracts off today's Kenyan pump price is pricing off a policy, not a cost.

What everyone is missing, then, is the direction of travel, and it is knowable now. The intelligent response is not to wait for the October review and react; it is to act while the props still hold.

For city hotels — where the electricity pass-through, not the pump, is the true margin killer — the move is to read the pass-through line on the bill rather than the advertised tariff band, and to lock energy-linked supplier and laundry contracts while the shilling is stable at around 129 to the dollar. A stable currency means you can no longer rely on drift to quietly absorb cost inflation; rate discipline has to be deliberate.

For bush and safari lodges, diesel is the cost engine — game drives, generators, transfers, supply runs. The flat pump price is a planning window, not a floor. Lock transfer and generator contracts now, before the October VAT decision, rather than after it.

For beach resorts and any operator running a multi-country book, the Kenya–Tanzania divergence is an allocation signal. Tanzania and Zanzibar's benign cost base is a structural margin advantage that is set to widen, not narrow. Weight net-rate sourcing, and the next increment of capital, accordingly.

None of this is a crisis. It is the opposite of drama: a set of dated, sourced, entirely foreseeable cost mechanics that reward the operator who reads the policy behind the price. The number that has not moved is the one worth watching hardest — because the reason it has not moved is on a timer.
