✈️ UGANDA AIRLINES TAKES ON RWANDAIR — DAILY KIGALI, AND A WEST AFRICA GATEWAY

City hotels in both capitals should read this as cheaper, more frequent regional connectivity feeding corporate and MICE demand, not a threat. Second, **Accra opens a West African feed** into Entebbe — trade, cargo and business travel from a region East Africa is barely connected to. That positions Kampala as a genuine transit-and-meeting point between West and East Africa: a forward demand signal worth building corporate rate around, especially into FHS Africa Kampala next February.

## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**RwandAir just lost a monopoly — and Kampala gained a gateway**

For years, if you wanted to fly non-stop between Kampala and Kigali, you flew RwandAir. That quietly changed this afternoon. At the Sheraton in Kampala, Uganda Airlines' acting chief executive Girma Wake confirmed the carrier will begin daily Entebbe–Kigali service on 18 November — and, in the same announcement and easy to miss, four weekly flights from Entebbe to Accra from 27 October. Kigali runs on the airline's CRJ-900 regional jets; Accra will be flown by a Boeing 737-800 wet-leased from Ethiopian Airlines.

It is tempting to file this under aviation trade news and move on. Don't. Route decisions are demand decisions, and both of these tell an East African operator something worth planning around.

Start with Kigali. The Kampala–Kigali corridor is one of the busiest business routes in the East African Community, and until now a single carrier owned the non-stop version of it. A second daily service does three things at once: it adds seats, it introduces genuine fare competition, and it makes a same-day return trip realistic for a wider slice of corporate travellers. For city hotels in both capitals, that is not a threat — it is a tailwind. Cheaper, more frequent connectivity between two EAC capitals feeds exactly the short-stay corporate and meetings demand that fills midweek city rooms. The property that benefits is the one whose corporate sales desk is already talking to Kigali-based accounts about easier Kampala access, rather than waiting to see what the timetable does.

Accra is the more interesting decision strategically. West Africa is one of the least directly connected regions from East Africa, despite deepening trade and investment ties, and a four-times-weekly link into Entebbe starts to close that gap. Uganda Airlines is explicit that it sees Accra as a business, tourism and cargo play. For Kampala, the second-order effect is what matters: the city becomes a plausible transit-and-meeting point between West and East Africa. That is a forward demand signal — the kind of structural connectivity that underwrites a case for corporate rate discipline and for bidding on regional conferences. It lands, usefully, ahead of FHS Africa Kampala next February, when the city will be making exactly that argument to hotel investors.

So what should an operator actually do this week? If you run city rooms in Kampala or Kigali, brief your corporate sales team now: the connectivity story has improved, and it is a reason to hold rate and pursue regional accounts, not to discount into new capacity. If you are a revenue manager, resist the assumption that today's Entebbe–Kigali fares will survive into the fourth quarter — a monopoly route rarely keeps its pricing once a second daily carrier arrives, and RwandAir will defend the corridor on schedule and on price. Model both the upside (more inbound seats) and the fare compression, rather than only the half you like.

There is a wider pattern here worth naming. Across the region, airlift is expanding faster than rate discipline can absorb it — new frequencies, new carriers on old routes, new long-haul gateways — and the operators who win from that are the ones who treat every new route as a demand input to plan against, not a headline to admire. More seats are only good news for the property that has decided in advance what it will do with them.

The monopoly that ended today was small and specific. The habit it should prompt is not: read the timetable like a booking curve, and act before the doors open.
