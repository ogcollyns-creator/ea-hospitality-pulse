## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**East Africa is building rooms faster than it is selling 2027.**

The Seychelles delegation at IFTM Top Resa in Paris this month was not large, but it was unusually complete. Nine partners stood behind the Seychelles Tourism Board from 15 to 17 September: Hilton, Meliá, Raffles and Savoy, three destination management companies, a specialist operator, and the national airline. Bernadette Willemin, Special Advisor for Tourism to the Minister, led it in person, and what she said deserves reading slowly by anyone running a beach property between Diani and Nungwi.

"The 2027 planning cycle is being decided now, and for this market it is decided with the French trade," she said, in comments reported on 21 September 2026.

Guillaume Albert, chief executive of Creole Travel Services, was specific about the outcome. "Several of our major French partners are positioning Seychelles as a strategic destination for 2027 as they look to diversify their long-haul island offering," he said.

Diversify from what, exactly. The set of Indian Ocean islands a French operator can put into a long-haul winter programme is short, and Zanzibar is on it. A buyer who says they are diversifying is describing a reallocation, not an expansion. Somebody's allocation is being moved.

The same week gave Seychelles a second, harder-to-reverse win. Condor's weekly Frankfurt nonstop became year-round on 19 September, closing a gap that had run from mid-May. In five days a competitor secured trade positioning in one major European market and permanent lift in the other.

Our own September went on tax notices, tariffs and entry orders: all real, all fought at home, and none of them a decision about who sells our rooms next year.

W Hospitality Group's 2026 Africa pipeline report, released 10 March, counted a record 123,846 rooms across 675 hotels. The number that should interest an East African owner is not the pipeline's size but how much has already been poured.

Kenya has 6,190 pipeline rooms across 35 properties, of which 4,922 are under construction. That is 79.5%. Ethiopia sits at 79.9%, Tanzania at 77.5%. On the same table, Nigeria converts at 39.2% and Cape Verde at 8.6%.

Trevor Ward, managing director of W Hospitality Group, read it as a strength. "Kenya, Ethiopia and Tanzania show some of the highest construction ratios on the continent, which suggests that this is where we are likely to see new supply coming through in the short to medium term," he said.

He is right, and that is the problem. A hotel at 8.6% conversion is a call option someone can shelve. A hotel at 79.5% conversion is a liability with a completion date: steel bought, contractor on site, debt drawing down, opening a scheduling question rather than a strategic one.

The industry has spent six months reading that ratio as a confidence signal. It is at least equally a deadline. Those rooms open whatever the trade decides this autumn, and the trade is deciding this autumn. Demand is not matching the pace: UN Tourism has global arrivals up 0.4% in the first half of 2026, full-year forecast cut to 1% to 2%.

Why a neighbour's good week is our problem is best explained by an operator. Mohammed Hersi, group director of operations at Pollmans Tours and Safaris, was asked on 20 September why the wider industry should care what happens to Zanzibar and the Mara. "People come to the Mara and then add Diani or Zanzibar. Or they come to Zanzibar and add two or three days on safari in the Serengeti or Manyara," he said. "The moment these places lose their shine, the pull weakens across the board."

Read that alongside Albert's line and the exposure becomes clear. East Africa does not sell beach and bush as separate products to the European trade. It sells a pairing, and the pairing is the defence. An operator who books a client into Zanzibar has a commercial reason to sell the Serengeti leg too. One who books that client into Seychelles has no safari to attach. The substitution costs Zanzibar one beach week and costs a Tanzanian or Kenyan camp the three nights that travelled with it.

That is what is being missed. The threat to East African beach stock is not priced in beach terms. It is priced in bush nights, and it lands on operators who will never see a Seychelles rate card.

Hersi's prescription was yield rather than volume: Zanzibar "should sit medium to upper luxury rather than chase mass numbers, because there is only one Zanzibar." That is a call for rate discipline, and the supply data says it is about to get harder to hold. A market absorbing thousands of contracted rooms has every incentive to buy occupancy with price, and the first to break usually break in the shoulder.

So: beach operators should treat the European trade calendar as a fixed cost, not a marketing option. Bush operators should ask their inbound agents what share of 2027 European safari bookings still carries a Zanzibar or Diani extension, because that ratio moves months before any arrivals statistic. City hotels have the clearest window: Kenya's densest conference weeks run to mid-October, before the new stock opens. Hold rate through it.

The rooms are coming, decided three years ago by credit committees that are not taking calls now. What is still open is who sells them, and that is being settled this autumn, with or without us in the room.
