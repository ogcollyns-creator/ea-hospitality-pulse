## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Two East African governments moved on the tourism value chain today. Both were really asking the same question: who controls it, and who keeps the money.**

In Nairobi, the Tourism (Amendment) Bill 2026 ran into its first serious friction. The Bill would fold two specialist state agencies — the Tourism Research Institute and the Tourism Finance Corporation — into the Kenya Tourism Board, consolidating research, market intelligence and financing under one roof in the name of efficiency. On 21 August the National Assembly's Tourism and Wildlife Committee, chaired by Kareke Mbiuki, pushed back on absorbing the research institute, with MPs from the coastal tourism seats of Kisauni, Lamu East and Voi arguing it should be adequately funded and left standing rather than merged away (Capital FM, 21 August). On the same day, the Ministry of Tourism floated a separate change: splitting the collection of the 2% Tourism Fund levy from the management of the fund itself, and widening the Cabinet Secretary's oversight of both (allAfrica / Capital FM, 21 August).

Read together, these are not two housekeeping amendments. They are a move to centralise control of the sector's information, its financing and its levy revenue — and a reminder that "streamlining" and "control" are often the same sentence read from different ends.

For operators, the research point is the one to watch closely. The Tourism Research Institute is where Kenya's arrivals-by-source-market and bed-night data come from — the closest thing the market has to official occupancy signal. Revenue managers use it to forecast yield; DMCs use it to decide which source markets to build product for; investors use it to underwrite pipeline. An independent, adequately funded research body produces numbers that everyone, including the government's own critics, can cite. A research function absorbed into the national marketing board — the very body with an interest in an optimistic story — is a weaker instrument, whatever the org chart says. The committee's instinct to fund rather than fold is, from a data-quality standpoint, the commercially sensible one.

The levy change is a cash-flow and marketing-spend question dressed as governance. The 2% Tourism Fund levy on hotels and restaurants is not new money; what is being redrawn is who collects it, on what timetable, and who directs where it goes. Tourism Fund money underwrites destination marketing and MICE bid support — the spending that fills rooms two and three years out. Any operator planning GOPPAR, or counting on Fund-supported marketing to drive a shoulder season, should treat the disbursement rules as a live variable, not a fixed input, until the Bill settles.

Four hundred kilometres south, Zanzibar answered the same value-chain question with enforcement rather than legislation. The Zanzibar Commission for Tourism, working with Immigration, arrested three foreign nationals — including the Kenyan businesswoman Esther Mutheu Samuka — on 18 August for running tour and excursion operations reserved for Zanzibari citizens under the Tourism Act No. 6 of 2009 (Citizen Digital, 21 August). The island's position is unambiguous: foreign capital is welcome in the beds, but the ground product — the excursions, the spice tours, the transfers — belongs to locals.

That is a direct operating risk for any regional DMC or coastal hotel selling Zanzibar as part of a multi-country itinerary. If the excursion leg of your package is run by a foreign-owned entity rather than a licensed Zanzibari operator, you are exposed — to the arrest of a partner, to a guest stranded mid-stay, and to the transfer liability that follows. The fix is unglamorous and urgent: audit your island partners' licensing now, before the European peak, not during it.

The common thread is worth sitting with. Across the region, governments are re-asserting control over who owns the tourism value chain and who captures its revenue — through mergers and levies in Kenya, through ownership enforcement in Zanzibar. None of it is hostile to investment. All of it changes the operating map. The operators who read these moves early, brief their agents, verify their partners and write the changes into net-rate contracts will trade through them. The ones who treat them as background noise will meet them at a checkout desk, or a police station.
