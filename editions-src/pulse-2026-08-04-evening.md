## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**The travel advisory you weren't watching**

Everyone in East African hospitality has spent the summer watching Uganda. The US State Department put it at Level 4 — Do Not Travel — over the regional Ebola outbreak, and that headline has dominated every risk conversation from Nairobi to Kigali. Meanwhile, a full country over, a quieter fact sat unattended: since 31 October 2025, the United States has rated **Tanzania at Level 3 — Reconsider Travel.**

I'll start with a correction, because getting this right matters more than looking polished. Our own editions earlier this week carried Tanzania and Zanzibar at Level 2. That was wrong. The level was raised to 3 more than nine months ago, on the grounds of unrest, crime, terrorism concentrated in the far-south Mtwara Region, and the targeting of gay and lesbian travellers. We've verified the current status against travel.state.gov and corrected our board. A brief that won't correct itself in public isn't worth reading.

Now the intelligence, because the correction is also the story. The regional US advisory board now reads Kenya Level 2, Tanzania Level 3, Uganda Level 4, Rwanda Level 3. Tanzania sits a full step above Kenya — and it has for nine months, largely unremarked, because the market's attention was elsewhere.

Here is what most operators are missing: the **divergence between source markets.** The United States says Reconsider Travel. The United Kingdom, looking at the same country, maintains Level 1 — no advisory against travel at all. That is not a rounding error; it is two of your largest source markets forming opposite impressions of the same destination. Your American guests are reading "Reconsider Travel" on an official government page. Your British guests are reading nothing of the kind.

For an operator, that divergence is a pricing and messaging instruction, not a crisis. The risk drivers behind Tanzania's Level 3 — mainland demonstrations, urban crime, and a terrorism concern that sits in the deep south near the Mozambique border — are concentrated hundreds of kilometres from the northern safari circuit and from Zanzibar's beaches. That is a factual, defensible thing to be able to say to a nervous American client, calmly, before they cancel. It is not a reason to discount; it is a reason to inform. The properties that lose bookings in an advisory environment are the ones whose front desk is caught flat-footed by a question they should have rehearsed.

So the practical work is unglamorous and specific. Segment your advisory messaging by source market: a proactive, factual note for US agents and direct US guests; nothing special for the UK trade, who face no such warning. Make sure your reservations team can explain, in two sentences, why a Level 3 driven by mainland unrest does not describe the Serengeti crater rim or a Nungwi beach. And check your own marketing — if you've been quoting a "no major advisories" line into the US market, it's now inaccurate.

The deeper lesson is about discipline. This error survived in our board for months because a status level was maintained by hand and never cross-checked against the source. We've now automated that cross-check — the board is compared to the State Department feed daily, and any mismatch is flagged before it can go out. In an intelligence product, the unglamorous machinery that catches your own mistakes is the product. Anyone can publish confidence. The value is in publishing things that are still true when a client acts on them.
