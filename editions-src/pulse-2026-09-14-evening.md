## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**The most expensive thing about Kenya's frozen fuel price is that it didn't move**

Kenya's energy regulator announced this afternoon that maximum pump prices will not change between 15 September and 14 October. Super petrol stays at KSh 214.03 a litre in Nairobi, diesel at KSh 217.86, kerosene at KSh 191.38.

Tomorrow's coverage will call this relief. It is closer to a deferral, and hospitality operators should read it that way.

Beneath the price, nothing was flat. Between July and August the average landed cost of imported diesel rose 11.86 per cent, from US$855.59 to US$957.05 per cubic metre. Kerosene rose 9.71 per cent. Petrol went the other way, falling 7.87 per cent. Those are EPRA's own figures, published today. Meanwhile Brent settled at US$101.21 on 9 September — its highest close since May, and roughly 30 per cent above early-August lows, on disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. For a sense of pace: the Murban benchmark the Central Bank of Kenya carried in its 3 July bulletin was US$67.99.

Three import costs moved, two of them sharply upward, and not one pump price changed.

**The direction of the cross-subsidy is the part worth noticing**

A frozen price in a moving market is a transfer. Someone gives up a fall so that someone else avoids a rise. This month the petrol motorist surrendered a cut to fund the hold on diesel and kerosene.

For hotels, camps and lodges that transfer ran in our favour, which is unusual enough to say plainly. Hospitality does not run on petrol. It runs on diesel — game-drive vehicles and airport transfers, standby generators, borehole pumps, laundry boilers, the truck that brings everything up a murram road. Kenya's August inflation data makes the exposure explicit: transport prices rose 15.7 per cent year on year while restaurant and accommodation prices rose 3.0 per cent. The cost of delivering a guest is inflating at roughly five times the rate at which the room can be repriced.

But a transfer is not an abolition. Kenya deployed KSh 938 million from the Petroleum Development Levy Fund in the cycle now ending, and KSh 945 million in the one before. A fund drawn on monthly, against an import cost rising monthly, resolves in only one direction.

There is a date attached to that resolution, and it is not widely diarised. The concessionary 8 per cent VAT rate on petroleum products expires on 14 October — the same day EPRA announces the next review. A deferred import cost and a tax step-up are scheduled to arrive in the same notice.

**The regional picture moved too — the other way**

While Kenya froze, Tanzania cut. EWURA lowered capped petrol by 102 shillings a litre and diesel by 101, effective 2 September, taking Dar es Salaam diesel to TZS 3,877. Uganda, which does not cap at all, passed the global move straight through: petrol up 28.5 per cent year on year in August, kerosene up 31.2 per cent.

Three neighbouring markets, three mechanisms, three directions in the same month. The practical consequence is that a Serengeti transfer and a Maasai Mara transfer are now priced off different fuel curves, and the gap widened in September without a single regional headline registering it. Anyone selling a cross-border circuit on one blended fuel assumption is quietly mispricing a leg of it.

**What to do in the next thirty days**

For bush properties, the freeze is a planning window, not a saving. You know your diesel cost until 14 October and not one day further. Re-quote fourth-quarter transfer supplements and generator surcharges against KSh 217.86 now, and decline to sell a fixed transfer rate into November without a fuel-adjustment clause.

For city hotels the pump is the lesser exposure; the meter is the larger one. Kenya's electricity pass-through charges alone exceeded KSh 5.00 per kilowatt-hour in the July billing cycle, and they move every month with almost no coverage.

For coast properties, Mombasa prints roughly three shillings a litre below Nairobi across all three grades. With the Africa MICE Summit arriving in Mombasa on 1 October, the instinct will be to discount into the shoulder. The better trade is to hold rate and let the cheaper delivered fuel do the margin work.

One honest note. On 15 August we predicted this review would push Nairobi diesel above KSh 217.86, reasoning that the stabilisation fund was near-exhausted. It printed at 217.86. We were wrong about the fund's capacity — not about the pressure, which is now a month larger and still unpaid.
