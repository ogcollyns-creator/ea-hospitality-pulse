## LINKEDIN BIG READ

East Africa is winning Africa's hotel construction race — and that is precisely why its operators should be planning their defence now, not celebrating.

The numbers first. Africa's hotel development pipeline reached a record 123,846 rooms across 675 hotels in 2026, up 18.6% year on year, according to the W Hospitality Group's annual Hotel Chain Development Pipelines in Africa report (released March 2026). Egypt and Morocco still dominate the raw volume. But the report's sharper finding is about execution, not signatures: East Africa leads the continent in rooms actually under construction. Nearly 80% of Kenya's 6,190 pipeline rooms and Ethiopia's 5,964 are being built right now; Tanzania sits at 77.5%. Across much of Africa the "pipeline" is a wish list that never breaks ground. In East Africa, the cranes are up.

That distinction is the whole story, because it turns a soft signal into a hard date. A signed management agreement tells you a brand likes your market. A room under construction tells you when your competitive set expands — and it is a date you can read off today. Roughly 65,000 rooms are scheduled to open across Africa in 2026 and 2027, and the East African share is disproportionately real. For an owner in Nairobi's Upper Hill, Kampala, Kigali, Dar es Salaam or along the Zanzibar coast, the question is not whether new branded supply is coming. It is which quarter it lands in your submarket.

Here is what the celebration is missing. New supply does not reprice a market when it stabilises two years after opening. It reprices the market the day it opens, when a 200-room international flag has debt to service and empty floors to fill, and buys occupancy the only way a new entrant can — on rate. Every existing property in that submarket inherits a new price anchor overnight. And this wave arrives at an awkward moment for pricing power: the fastest-growing demand in the region is the regional, cross-border traveller, structurally more resilient than long-haul but also more price-sensitive. More rooms chasing a value-conscious guest is the textbook setup for a margin squeeze — and it is being written into concrete now.

The defensive move is not to match the newcomer's launch rate; that is a race you lose to a balance sheet deeper than yours. It is to take business off the table before the new supply can bid for it. Three plays, by segment.

City hotels are at the centre of the wave, with the most to lose and gain. Contract your 2026 and 2027 corporate, group and MICE business now, at today's rates, on multi-night and annual terms, so that when a new flag opens and discounts, a meaningful share of your demand is already booked and immune. Compete on what a brand-new building cannot buy on day one: an established regional corporate book, a proven events and F&B operation, and staff who know your repeat guests by name.

Bush and safari lodges are the least exposed, because the branded pipeline is overwhelmingly city product. That is an opportunity, not merely a relief. When city hotels discount, the premium guest's total trip cost falls, freeing budget to trade up on the safari leg. Hold your rate, sell scarcity, and let the city price war subsidise your yield.

Beach resorts sit between the two. Zanzibar and the wider coast are drawing real branded resort supply, and the same logic applies: lock advance group and wholesale contracts, and differentiate on product and location before the new inventory competes for the same charter demand.

The pipeline data is genuinely good news for East Africa — it is a bet by the world's biggest operators that the region's demand is durable. But a bet on rooms is not a bet on your rate. The operators who come through the 2026–27 opening wave with pricing power intact will be the ones who read the construction schedule as a countdown, and acted while the clock still favoured them.
