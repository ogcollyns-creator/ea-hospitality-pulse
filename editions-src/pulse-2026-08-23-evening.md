## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Tanzania's new electricity surplus won't reach its most valuable tourism market. Here's what that means for your power budget.**

On 22 August 2026, Tanzania switched on the 2,115MW Julius Nyerere Hydropower Project. Installed generation now stands at 4,646MW against peak demand of roughly 2,271MW — a surplus of about 2,375MW (TanzaniaInvest, 23 August 2026). In a region where hoteliers still treat diesel gensets as a permanent line in the opex, a neighbour going structurally power-long is not an energy-page story. It is a competitiveness story.

But the surplus stops at the water — and that is the part worth reading twice.

Zanzibar, the country's highest-value tourism zone, cannot physically take the mainland's new abundance. Unguja is fed by two submarine cables: a 45MW line from 1980 running at 98.8% of capacity, and a 100MW line at 97.3%. Together they leave the islands with about 135MW of reliable supply against an estimated 65MW deficit (The Citizen TZ / AfDB). Zanzibar Electricity Corporation plans to lift the interconnector from 132kV to 220kV and lay a new 270MW cable, with an 18MW Makunduchi solar plant due to come online — but until that transmission arrives, a beach resort on Unguja sees none of the generation windfall being celebrated on the mainland.

The bottleneck has moved from generation to transmission. That single shift rewrites the competitiveness map inside Tanzania itself.

**What most operators will miss:** the mainland surplus is a mainland benefit. City hotels in Dar es Salaam, Arusha and Dodoma, and the gateway-town logistics that feed the northern safari circuit, stand to gain the most — lower load-shedding risk, less reliance on backup diesel, a steadier cold chain. Island resorts gain nothing this season. A Zanzibar general manager who reads "Tanzania has a 2,375MW surplus" and trims the 2027 genset fuel budget would be making a planning error on the basis of a true headline.

Segment it plainly. City properties are the winners: revisit backup-diesel assumptions for 2027, but do not price in a tariff cut that has not been announced — surplus generation is not the same as cheaper units on your meter. Bush lodges, largely off-grid on solar and diesel, gain little directly; their upside is reliability in the towns they stage through. Beach resorts should hold genset capex and fuel budgets exactly where they are until the interconnector upgrade is commissioned. Their power risk is unchanged.

Now widen the lens. While mainland Tanzania moves toward energy abundance, Kenya is moving the other way on cost. EPRA's monthly pass-through charges — the fuel energy cost charge plus the forex adjustment — reached a combined KSh4.70 per kWh in August 2026 (Kenyans.co.ke / Breaking Kenya News, 18 August 2026), sitting on top of Nairobi diesel at KSh217.86 a litre (EPRA, 14 August 2026). Tanzanian inflation runs at 4.0% against Kenya's 6.4% (NBS / KNBS). The energy and cost-base gap between mainland Tanzania and Kenya is widening, and it is widening in Tanzania's favour. For any operator benchmarking mainland ADR competitiveness against Nairobi or the Kenyan coast, that is a quiet structural tailwind — not a headline, but a margin.

The forward call worth writing down: mainland Tanzania's structural power position improves through 2027, Zanzibar's does not materially change until the new interconnector lands, and Kenya's per-unit electricity cost keeps drifting up on the monthly pass-through. Budget to the grid you are actually connected to — not to the one making the news.
