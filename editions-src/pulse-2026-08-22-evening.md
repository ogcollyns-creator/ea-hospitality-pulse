## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Seventy thousand Ebola vaccine doses reached the Democratic Republic of the Congo this week. For East Africa's safari operators, the temptation is to read that as the beginning of the end. It isn't — and mistaking a research milestone for a containment one will cost you a season.**

Here are the facts, in order. On 20 August the UN's International Coordinating Group on vaccine provision allocated 70,000 doses of the Ervebo vaccine to the DRC: 20,000 for a Phase 3 clinical trial and 50,000 for frontline health workers, a move the World Health Organization and Africa CDC both welcomed. Ervebo is a real vaccine with a real record — against the Zaire strain of Ebola, the virus behind the 2014–16 West African epidemic. The outbreak now burning through eastern DRC is a different pathogen: the Bundibugyo virus. WHO was explicit this week that whether Ervebo protects against Bundibugyo "remains unknown," with only early laboratory and animal data to suggest it might. That is precisely why 20,000 of the doses are going into a trial rather than a campaign. The headline says vaccines have arrived. The epidemiology says a research question is being asked, not answered.

The scale of the problem those doses are meant to touch is sobering. As of 19 August the DRC had recorded 5,290 confirmed cases and 2,516 deaths — the country's deadliest Ebola outbreak on record and the fastest-growing anywhere, ever — with 81 new cases confirmed in a single day (ECDC, 21 August). Ituri province is the epicentre at 4,447 cases; the virus has crept into North Kivu and Haut-Uele. More than 160 health workers have been infected and 40 have died. And the response is being run through an active war: the M23 insurgency is blocking medical teams, closing health centres and making the contact-tracing and safe burials that end Ebola outbreaks close to impossible. A vaccine of uncertain efficacy, deployed into a conflict zone, does not flip this trajectory in a quarter.

For hospitality, the transmission has never really been viral. It is regulatory and reputational. The United States holds Uganda at Level 4, "Do Not Travel," and Rwanda at Level 3, "Reconsider Travel" — both pinned to the DRC epidemic next door. This is the part most commentary gets wrong: Uganda earned neither. Kampala declared its own small Bundibugyo flare-up over weeks ago — 20 cases, two deaths, every transmission chain traced and closed — and remains on active surveillance for imported cases. Uganda's gorilla-tourism economy, one of the country's hardest foreign-currency earners, is being priced for its neighbour's crisis. And nothing about a trial-stage vaccine gives a risk-averse State Department the epidemiological cover to downgrade. The advisory is likely to outlast the news cycle by many months.

So the operator question is not "when does the vaccine fix this." It is "how do I trade through a long advisory trough." For Ugandan bush properties around Bwindi and Queen Elizabeth, and for Rwanda's Volcanoes lodges, that means three things: build the trough into your 2027 forecasts rather than betting on a Q4 rebound; shift marketing weight onto regional and non-US source markets that don't move on a State Department cable; and keep visible, factual screening and a holding line ready for every agent enquiry. For city and beach properties in Kenya, Tanzania and Zanzibar, the job is the opposite and just as urgent — pre-empt the guest who conflates "Ebola in East Africa" with your address. The outbreak is in eastern DRC, well over a thousand kilometres from the Kenyan coast and the Zanzibar beaches, and no advisory covering those destinations has moved on Ebola grounds. Say both halves of that sentence, clearly, before a headline says the first half for you.

The vaccine is welcome. It is also, for now, a laboratory event with a hopeful press release attached. Manage the perception, because the pathogen was never coming for your bookings — the advisory was.
