The demonstration that should worry East African lodge owners lasted a few seconds and was about an aeroplane. On 14 October, in Seattle, Mike Fridgen showed what his new company's software had done with one of his own bookings. A fare on an Alaska Airlines flight had fallen after he paid for it. The software noticed, captured the difference as a credit, and told him. A video shown alongside it repriced another flight and saved $180.

Fridgen's company is called Vayo, and it is not a startup in the ordinary sense. It launched that day as a wholly new venture of Booking Holdings, the owner of Booking.com, Agoda, Priceline, Kayak and OpenTable, which said it planned to put $100 million behind it, subject to the product's progress and market conditions. Glenn Fogel, the group's chief executive, framed the logic in a sentence worth reading twice by anyone who sells rooms: "Vayo's openness allows it to create value regardless of where a reservation was made."

What Vayo does is narrow and, for that reason, unusually consequential. It does not currently sell hotel rooms. It connects to a traveller's email inbox, reads the travel confirmations and nothing else, assembles them into one itinerary, and then keeps watching. It watches for flight disruption and flags what a delay does to a check-in. It watches for rooms opening at hotels that were sold out. And it watches prices. When a hotel's rate falls after a guest has booked, Vayo tells her, and prompts her to act. Automated capture of hotel price drops, the thing it already does with some flights, is promised once transactional booking arrives, which Fridgen said would be a matter of weeks. He described the ambition as "complete coverage of supply", and noted that most travellers "don't book in any single source".

Strip away the product language and the proposition is a surveillance layer over the confirmed booking, built and funded by the largest seller of hotel rooms in Europe, pointed at every rate in the market including the ones its own brands did not sell. White Sky Hospitality, reviewing the week's distribution news on 19 October, put the shift in one line: "The booking is no longer the asset. The itinerary is."

East African operators have good reason to treat this as their problem rather than a Seattle curiosity, and the reason is the shape of their own rate cards.

Look at how the region sells its low season. The Safari Collection, which runs Giraffe Manor in Nairobi, Sala's Camp in the Mara and Sasaab in Samburu, publishes a two-tier calendar. Travel between 1 June and 31 October falls in what it calls The Season. Travel between 6 January and 31 May, and again from 1 November to 15 December, falls in Savings Season, and the published discounts there run from 30 per cent off a four-night stay to 40 per cent off ten nights. That is not an aberration. It is the regional norm, the standard answer to the long rains and the short rains, and every serious camp from Laikipia to Zanzibar's east coast has a version of it. Rates fall by a third or more, publicly, on a schedule the whole market knows.

Now set that against what the research says a price cut does to bookings already on the books.

The most directly relevant study appeared in the International Journal of Hospitality Management in February, written by Martyna Kmiecik and Nuno Antonio of NOVA Information Management School in Lisbon under a title that leaves little to interpretation: "Cancel, rebook, save: Revenue leakage from price cuts in hotels." They took 1,973,137 reservations from 628 Portuguese properties covering 2022 to 2024 and looked for a specific behaviour: a guest cancelling a booking at a property and immediately rebooking the same property at a lower rate.

The incidence is small. Rebookings of that kind accounted for 0.94 per cent of reservations and 7.55 per cent of all cancellations. The trend is the part that matters. The rate rose from 0.67 per cent in 2022 to 0.79 per cent in 2023 and 1.09 per cent in 2024, roughly a doubling in two years, in a period when nobody had yet built a machine to do the watching. Around 80 per cent of rebookings happened within a month of the original booking, and nearly half within a week.

The cost per event is where an East African operator should stop and do the arithmetic. Across the affected properties, revenue fell by €67.03 per reservation, a drop of 16.2 per cent, and by €22.63 per night, a drop of 17.1 per cent. Gross displacement across the sample came to €1.24 million, averaging €2,913.81 at each of the 426 hotels that experienced it. Four properties lost more than €30,000 each.

And then the detail that converts this from a European curiosity into an East African warning. Kmiecik and Antonio broke the per-night loss down by accommodation type. Rural hotels lost the most: €43.15 per night, nearly double the €22.04 lost by ordinary hotels. Inns lost €39.00, holiday resorts €32.08. The pattern is intuitive once stated. The properties that bleed hardest from a cancel-and-rebook are the remote, high-rate, low-inventory ones, where a single night carries a large absolute value and there is no volume to absorb the loss. A twelve-tent camp in the Mara is, in the language of that table, a rural hotel with a resort's nightly rate.

The causal direction has independent support. In Cornell Hospitality Quarterly, Gloria Urrea, Xiao Huang and Dan Zhang examined 15,101 bookings at a high-end American hotel, of which 3,141, or 20.8 per cent, were cancelled. They found that a $50 increase in the price paid raised the hazard of cancellation by 16 per cent, and that continued price searching after booking, tested against competitor rates, mediated 27 per cent of that effect. Their counterfactual estimate is the number to carry into a budget meeting: a hotel that ignores the pricing effect could forgo up to 11 per cent of high-season revenue.

A third dataset narrows it further. Vicente Ramos Mir, Bartolome Deya Tortella, Veronica Leoni and Juan Luis Nicolau, writing in Tourism Management in June, analysed more than two million bookings across 327 hotels in Mallorca for stays from 2021 to 2024. Twenty-two per cent of bookings were cancelled. When a comparable hotel nearby became €10 cheaper, cancellation became 4.7 per cent more likely. Non-refundable bookings were 82 per cent less likely to cancel than fully refundable ones. And bookings made through online travel agencies were 72 per cent more likely to be cancelled than bedbank bookings, while direct bookings were 42 per cent less likely. The channel, the authors note, can simply shift a booking from one hotel to another, which means the cancellation lands on the property rather than on the intermediary.

Hold those three findings together and the mechanism is plain. Cancellation is not weather. It is a response to price, it concentrates in refundable bookings, and it is strongest where the guest is still shopping. Everything about an East African safari booking maximises all three. The booking is made months out, on refundable terms, through an agent or an OTA, for a product whose published rate is scheduled to fall by a third in the very months the guest might otherwise have paid full price.

Until now the sector's protection was friction. A guest who had paid a deposit in February for an August trip had to think to go back and re-shop, and most did not. That friction is what Vayo removes. It does not ask the traveller to return. It already holds her itinerary, and it checks.

There is evidence travellers will let it. Mews surveyed 3,250 travellers across eight markets in July and found that 63 per cent were comfortable with AI tracking hotel rates on their behalf, against 44 per cent comfortable letting it plan an itinerary and 41 per cent letting it personalise a room. The research is vendor-commissioned and skewed towards Europe and the United States, and it was fielded before this month's launches, so treat the level with caution and the ranking as the finding. Guests will delegate the chore of watching the price. They will not delegate the choice of where to stay. Price-watching happens to be precisely the chore that exposes a lodge's own discount to its own confirmed guests.

Nobody in the industry can claim surprise. Naveen Manga, Marriott's chief information officer, said of the post-booking re-shop this month: "As an industry, we allowed that." In Europe the structural complaint is already formal. HOTREC's 2026 distribution study, covering 2,713 properties, found that Booking Holdings and Expedia control more than 85 per cent of European hotel bookings made through OTAs, with Booking Holdings alone at almost 69 per cent, and that 51 per cent of hotels say OTAs undercut their prices frequently or occasionally, eight percentage points more than in 2023. "Growing concentration must not translate into growing dependency," said Alexandros Vassilikos, HOTREC's president. East Africa has no equivalent study, no equivalent association filing and no equivalent regulator looking at it.

What it has, as of this month, is enthusiasm. Opening the sixteenth Magical Kenya Travel Expo on 7 October under the theme of digital transformation and artificial intelligence, with more than 400 exhibitors and more than 10,000 delegates, Tourism Cabinet Secretary Rebecca Miano told the room that "the next competitive advantage belongs to destinations that harness technology to understand travellers better". She is not wrong. The uncomfortable part is that the best-capitalised AI in travel this month is not pointed at understanding travellers on behalf of destinations. It is pointed at a lodge's rate calendar on behalf of a guest who has already paid.

The remedies are unglamorous and they exist. Kmiecik and Antonio set them out at the end of their paper, and they read as a specification for the East African green season. Cap public downward rate moves at ten to fifteen per cent inside a defined window before arrival, and require sign-off above that. Move tactical discounts into fenced channels, member rates, closed groups, minimum stays, non-refundable structures, rather than onto the open shelf. Offer a credit or an upgrade to a guest who sees a lower price, which costs less than the cancellation. Audit parity daily across direct and OTA channels. And flag rebooking risk inside the reservation system, combining lead time, recent price trajectory and guest history, so someone can make a retention offer before the guest makes a decision.

None of that requires an operator to abandon seasonal pricing, which is the honest economics of a destination with two rainy seasons. It requires the discount to stop being a public broadcast to people who have already bought. A rate cut published to the open shelf in mid-November, in a market where a third of the guests holding refundable bookings are now represented by software that reads their inbox, is no longer only a demand tool. It is also a refund notice, issued automatically, to the guests who were already coming.

Vayo cannot yet rebook a room, and the gap between prompting a guest and completing the transaction is the whole of the sector's remaining breathing space. Fridgen says that gap closes in weeks. The green season starts on 1 November.
