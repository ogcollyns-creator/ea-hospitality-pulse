## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Kenya's power bill fell 54 cents this month. The same gazette round made leaving the grid permanently harder.**

**THE SITUATION**

On 18 September, Kenya's Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority gazetted the pass-through charges for every electricity meter reading taken in September: a Fuel Energy Cost Charge of KSh 3.00 per kWh, a foreign-exchange fluctuation adjustment of about KSh 1.14, and a Water Resource Management Authority levy of 1.48 cents. Together, KSh 4.16 per kWh.

August's notices, gazetted on 14 August, set the same three lines at KSh 3.51, KSh 1.1777 and KSh 0.015 — KSh 4.7027 in total.

The month-on-month move is a fall of 54 cents a unit, about 11.5 per cent. For a hotel that is not trivia. Electricity is the second or third largest controllable line in a full-service property, and in a 150-room city hotel running laundry, kitchen, chillers and meeting-space HVAC, 54 cents a unit compounds across six figures of annual consumption.

Most operators will read that as relief and move on. That would be the wrong read.

**WHAT EVERYONE IS MISSING**

Look at which line fell. Essentially all of the 54 cents came out of the fuel charge, which tracks thermal dispatch and moves every month in both directions. The forex adjustment — the structurally sticky line — barely moved, down around four cents. In August, independent power producers alone accounted for KSh 1.039 billion of the KSh 1.353 billion in sector exchange costs that line recovers. That exposure does not resolve because one month's thermal mix improved.

So the relief is real, and it is the reversible kind.

Now read the second notice. In the same week, EPRA amended the Schedule of Tariffs 2023 to provide that customers generating their own renewable power under net metering are credited for only half the electricity they export. It also defined feeding power into the Kenya Power network without approval as "dumping" — and provided that the exporter be billed for that power at the applicable base tariff.

Put the two notices side by side and the picture changes. The grid became 11.5 per cent cheaper for one month, in the line most likely to reverse. The alternative to the grid — rooftop solar with export — became structurally less attractive in the same gazette round, and now carries a compliance exposure for anyone whose inverter has been quietly exporting without an approved agreement.

For a decade, East African hospitality has treated self-generation as the hedge against utility volatility. That hedge has just been repriced by the regulator, in a week when the headline said prices were falling.

**THE SEGMENTED READ**

City hotels carry the highest kilowatt-hours per occupied room and take the largest absolute benefit this month — and the largest absolute hit when the fuel line turns. Recompute the energy line off the September reading, not August's, and do not write a one-month number into a 2027 rate card.

Bush lodges are the group most likely to be grid-tied with solar behind the meter. The compliance question is immediate and answerable this week: does the system export, and is there an approved net-metering agreement on file? A camp that discovers the answer when a bill arrives will be arguing from the weaker position.

Coastal properties on the Kenyan grid take the relief. Zanzibar properties do not — they sit on a separate utility and carry an infrastructure tax levied at two per cent of net electricity supply value, which no Kenyan gazette touches.

**WHAT TO DO IN THE NEXT THIRTY DAYS**

Reprice off September. Confirm your export status and your net-metering paperwork in writing. And when you sign anything running past 14 October, remember what else lands that day: Kenya's reduced 8 per cent VAT on petrol, diesel and kerosene expires, and EPRA's frozen pump cycle ends. A utility line that fell in September is not a reason to enter October without a cost-variation clause.
