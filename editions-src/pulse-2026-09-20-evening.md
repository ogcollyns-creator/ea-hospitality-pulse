The grid got cheaper for one month. Leaving the grid got permanently narrower.

## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Kenya's grid got cheaper. Leaving it got harder. Both happened in the same week.**

Joseph Siror had a warning, and it was not the one the room was expecting.

On 11 August, at Kenya Power's State of the Grid briefing in Nairobi, the utility's managing director set out what sounded like an engineering complaint. Wind and solar now supply 34 per cent of the country's energy mix at peak daytime demand of roughly 1,900MW. Global practice puts the comfortable ceiling at about 15 per cent of a grid's firm capacity.

"Our current system under the take-or-pay model of power purchase has led to an increase in VREs to over 20 per cent against a recommended average of 15 per cent," Siror said.

Then came the sentence every hotelier should have marked.

"Given the intermittent nature of wind and solar, we have no option but to dispatch and pay for generators, increasing the overall cost of power."

The national utility told the country, in a formal briefing, that the growth of self-generation is part of why electricity costs what it costs, and that the bill for stabilising around it lands on the consumer. Six weeks later, the regulation arrived.

On 18 September the Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority gazetted September's pass-through charges: a Fuel Energy Cost Charge of KSh 3.00 per kilowatt hour, a foreign exchange adjustment of about KSh 1.14 set against KSh 1.32 billion in sector exchange costs, and a Water Resource Management Authority levy of 1.48 cents. Together, KSh 4.16 a unit.

August's notices, gazetted on 14 August, had set the same three lines at KSh 3.51, KSh 1.1777 and KSh 0.015. Total: KSh 4.7027. A fall of 54 cents a unit, about 11.5 per cent.

In a 150 room city hotel running laundry, kitchen, chillers and meeting space air conditioning, that is visible in the September bill. The relief is real. It is also the most misleading number an operator will read this month.

Look at which line moved. Essentially the entire 54 cents came out of the fuel charge, the component that recovers thermal dispatch and swings every month in both directions. It is exactly the line Siror was describing in August. The forex adjustment, the structurally sticky component, barely moved, down around four cents. In August, independent power producers alone accounted for KSh 1.039 billion of the KSh 1.353 billion in sector exchange gains and losses that line recovers. One good month of dispatch does not touch that.

September is cheap in the reversible line and unchanged in the permanent one. The more consequential development is not in that notice at all.

In a separate notice the same week, EPRA amended the Schedule of Tariffs 2023. Customers generating their own renewable power under net metering are now credited, in the tariff schedule itself, for half the electricity they export. The amendment also defines feeding power into the Kenya Power network without approval as "dumping", and provides that the exporter be billed for that power at the applicable base tariff.

Neither provision is new law. The Energy (Net-Metering) Regulations 2024 already set that credit at 50 per cent of retail tariff for systems below 1MW. What changed is that it now sits in the tariff schedule, beside a billable penalty.

Put the two notices side by side and this is not housekeeping. It is a coherent position. The utility said in August that self-generation is what makes the grid expensive to stabilise. The regulator in September cut the cost of staying on the grid for one month, and confirmed in the tariff schedule that leaving it earns you half credit, with a billable penalty for doing it without paperwork.

For a decade, East African hospitality has treated self-generation as the hedge against utility volatility. That hedge has just been repriced by the regulator, in the same week the headline said prices were falling.

Kenya has by a wide margin the highest variable renewable dependence in the Eastern Africa Power Pool. Kenya Power put the comparison on the record in August: Egypt 10.4 per cent, Ethiopia 5.3 per cent, Uganda four per cent, Tanzania 1.2 per cent. Kenyan hospitality is furthest along a curve the rest of the region is only starting to climb, and the precedent being set in Nairobi about what an exported unit is worth to whoever generated it is the one Dar, Kampala and Kigali will inherit.

Three things worth doing this week. Recompute the energy line off the September reading rather than August's, and keep a one month number out of anything running into 2027.

Establish whether your system exports. Many properties with rooftop solar push surplus into the grid during low occupancy, particularly bush lodges and conference hotels with lumpy demand, and a good number never formalised a net metering agreement because the export was incidental. September gave that incidental export a name and a price, and a property that finds out when a bill arrives is negotiating from the weaker end of the table.

And watch 14 October, when Kenya's reduced 8 per cent fuel VAT expires and EPRA's frozen pump cycle ends on the same day. Anything signed now that runs past it needs a cost variation clause.

Siror offered one caveat worth keeping: battery storage could help with intermittency, he said, but would not entirely eliminate it. The grid's problem is not solved. It has been priced, and the price is shared between everyone connected to the network and everyone trying to leave it.

A fall of 54 cents a unit is a good month. It is not a strategy.
