## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**You sold this season in the dry. You may have to deliver it in the wet.**

On 8 September, Kenya's Ministry of Health activated a Health Sector El Niño Contingency Plan. The forecast behind it is a strong El Niño alongside a positive Indian Ocean Dipole — the pairing that preceded the floods of 1997 and 2019 — with parts of eastern Africa expected to be wetter than normal between October and December. Northern Kenya, Uganda, Rwanda and Burundi are named.

October to December is not an abstraction for anyone running a lodge, a camp or a coastal resort. It is the calving of the year: the shoulder that carries the Christmas book, the conference season in Nairobi and Kigali, the migration tail, the first serious European arrivals after the northern summer. Most of it has already been sold. Almost none of it has been priced for water.

Here is what everyone is missing. The public conversation about El Niño is a conversation about disaster — lives lost, families displaced, and rightly so. The operational conversation is quieter and it is about something else entirely: **access**. Rain does not usually close a lodge. It closes the last eleven kilometres of murram between the airstrip and the gate, and it does so on the afternoon a family of four is sitting in a Cessna Caravan over the Mara with a connecting flight out of Nairobi at 23:15.

The benchmark for what that looks like is not 1997. It is March to May this year. By 3 May, flooding had reached 27 counties, killed 122 people and affected 42,259, with roads, bridges, water systems and power damaged. That was the long rains, in a year no one had labelled an El Niño year.

Three things separate the operators who trade through a wet season from the ones who spend it processing refunds.

**The first is the road, not the roof.** Most properties inspect their buildings before the rains and their access after. Reverse it. Culverts, drifts, the airstrip apron and the single worst hundred metres on your approach are the assets that determine whether October revenue arrives. If you share that road with a neighbour, split the grader cost now; in November you will both be paying emergency rates for the same machine.

**The second is the protocol, not the apology.** An agent selling East Africa this quarter is choosing between properties. Send them, this month, a written wet-weather transfer protocol: what happens when the strip is unserviceable, who pays for the road transfer, what the rebooking window is, what your force majeure language actually says. Agents do not reward operators for good weather. They reward the ones who made the bad weather predictable. The property with a one-page protocol will hold its allocation; the one that improvises in November will quietly lose it in the 2027 contracting round.

**The third is water, and it is the one that becomes a reputation.** Flooding contaminates drinking water and sanitation systems — cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A. WHO's Kenya preparedness work names exactly this. Write down now, before you need it, the turbidity or rainfall threshold at which you switch to boiled or bottled water house-wide, who decides, and who tells the guests. A lodge that manages a water incident calmly is running a competent operation. A lodge that discovers the problem through a guest's stomach is running a news story.

There is a segmentation to this. Bush properties on unsealed access carry the acute risk and should be spending money on drainage this month. Coastal resorts face a different exposure: flight disruption, road access to Diani and Watamu, and the vector-borne health layer that three G7 governments have already flagged on Kenya's coast this year. City hotels are the near-term beneficiaries — Nairobi, Kampala and Kigali absorb the guests a washed-out circuit displaces, and the property with a clean, well-priced overnight product will take that business.

None of this is a forecast that it will flood. It is an observation that a government health ministry has moved from monitoring to a contingency plan, and that the commercial season sitting inside the forecast window is already on the books. The cost of preparing and being wrong is a graded road and a page of policy. The cost of not preparing and being right is the quarter.
