## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**Uganda's hotel brands are building through a "Do Not Travel" warning. Here's the bet they're making.**

Read the openings and Uganda looks like a market on the rise. This year Marriott placed its flagship brand in Kampala for the first time — a 277-unit twin-tower complex on Nsambya Hill, connected by a skybridge, opened in June. Asilia Africa, one of East Africa's most disciplined safari operators, is entering the country with Erebero Hills, an eight-suite lodge on the northern edge of Bwindi and its first move into the gorilla-trekking market. And the industry's investor and developer circuit has handed Kampala the 2027 edition of Future Hospitality Summit Africa, to be held at Speke Resort — the first time the region's leading hospitality-investment forum has convened in Uganda.

Three separate commitments to the same city inside twelve months. Then read the demand side, and the picture stops being a straight line up.

Uganda currently sits at United States Level 4 — Do Not Travel, raised on Ebola grounds, with a US restriction on inbound travellers who have been in the country within the prior 21 days. For an operator, Level 4 is not a footnote. It suppresses the long-haul corporate traveller, complicates group insurance, and gives every risk-averse Western booker a reason to choose elsewhere.

Here is what most commentary misses. The advisory penalty is not a verdict on Uganda's own health situation. Kampala declared its Bundibugyo outbreak over on 28 July — 20 confirmed cases, two deaths, every transmission chain reconstructed and closed. The Level 4 is pinned to the epidemic across the border in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, now the fastest-growing Ebola outbreak on record at roughly 5,000 cases and more than 2,300 deaths, which the WHO's emergency committee reviewed again on 18 August. Uganda is being priced for its neighbour's crisis, not its own.

That reframes the investment entirely. Marriott, Asilia and the FHS organisers are not betting on a recovery in Ugandan epidemiology — that recovery, on Uganda's own numbers, has already happened. They are betting that an externally imposed advisory penalty lifts once the DRC outbreak turns, and that when it does, a market with a maturing capital city and a supply-capped gorilla product will reprice quickly. It is a two-to-three-year call, made deliberately into the trough, when land, labour and construction are cheapest and the competitive field is thinnest.

Two cautions keep this honest. First, Uganda barely features in the continental hotel-construction pipeline reports that put Marriott alone at more than 31,000 rooms under way across Africa. This is conviction expressed one flagship signing at a time, not a building wave — a signal of selective confidence, not a market-wide surge. Second, the demand-side risk is real today even if it is temporary: a Level-4 window can outlast an outbreak, because advisories are lagging instruments.

For operators already in the market, the strategy writes itself. In Kampala, new branded rooms will divide a long-haul corporate pie that the advisory is actively shrinking — so win on regional corporate, government, NGO and UN business, and on forward-booked events like FHS 2027, rather than on a long-haul tide that has not yet returned. In the Bwindi corridor, Asilia's arrival raises the price ceiling and the service bar; permits are capped at US$800 and scarce, so hold rate, but diversify away from the single premium long-haul guest the advisory hits hardest. And for Kenya-coast and Zanzibar properties, Uganda's friction is quietly competitive: the long-haul East Africa itinerary that once ran through Entebbe is, for now, easier to sell through Nairobi or Zanzibar.

The openings are not a demand signal. They are a forward call on normalisation — and the operators who understand the difference will price for the market that is coming, not the one the advisory describes today.
