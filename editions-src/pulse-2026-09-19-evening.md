**1️⃣ THE UGANDA ENTRY BAN FINALLY PUBLISHED — WITH A DEADLINE NOBODY HERE IS WATCHING**

The CDC's 11 September Title 42 order was published on Wednesday as **91 FR 58673–58679, Docket No. CDC-2026-0892** *(Federal Register, 16 September 2026)*. On Tuesday we said it was six days old and unpublished. It suspends entry of covered aliens present in **DRC, Uganda or South Sudan in the previous 21 days** — and states: **"Written comments must be received on or before October 1, 2026."**

**WHAT EVERYONE IS MISSING**
The argument East Africa keeps making has already lost. The order's own **footnote 8** cites WHO's page announcing Uganda ended its outbreak after the 42-day countdown, accessed 10 September. CDC read it and kept Uganda in the operative clause anyway. "Uganda is clean" is not new to this agency. It is footnoted.

The argument that nearly landed is the one nobody here is making. A commenter said counting **airside transit** as "presence" is indefensible — a passenger who never clears immigration, in a disease not transmissible before symptoms. CDC **agreed on the science** and refused anyway, on operational grounds. A feasibility refusal is the kind that moves when someone shows the alternative is workable. And it bites hardest here: ninety minutes connecting through Entebbe becomes a 21-day US-entry disqualifier.

CDC received **three comments** on the August order. Three, worldwide.

🎯 **So what:** Screen your own people — any non-US-passport colleague or owner routing through EBB, Juba or Kinshasa inside 21 days of a US trip is inadmissible, airside included. Then file at regulations.gov, docket CDC-2026-0892, before 1 October. Argue transit, not Uganda-is-clean.

## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**East Africa has spent four months arguing that Uganda is Ebola-free. The US order that bans it says so too — in a footnote — and bans it anyway.**

On 16 September the Centers for Disease Control published its latest Title 42 order in the Federal Register: 91 FR 58673, Docket No. CDC-2026-0892. It continues the suspension of entry into the United States of foreign nationals present in the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Uganda or South Sudan at any point in the preceding 21 days. It took effect on 11 September and expires on 11 October.

Most of the region will read the headline and conclude nothing changed. What changed is that the document is now on the public record, with a comment period, and reading it tells you exactly which argument works and which does not.

**THE SITUATION**

Uganda's outbreak was declared over in July and certified by WHO in August: twenty confirmed cases, two deaths, last case on 21 June. The active outbreak is in the DRC, where WHO counted 7,404 confirmed cases and 3,577 deaths to 15 September, driven by the Bundibugyo species for which no licensed vaccine exists.

Every Ugandan trade body has spent the interval making the same case: Uganda is clean, and the ban should reflect it.

Footnote 8 of the published order cites WHO's own page, "Uganda ends Ebola outbreak following completion of 42-day countdown," accessed on 10 September. The agency read the evidence the region has been waving at it, footnoted it, and left Uganda in the operative clause.

**WHAT EVERYONE IS MISSING**

An argument formally acknowledged and then declined is not one that needs repeating louder. It is a closed question. Continuing to press it is not advocacy; it is a way of feeling busy.

The open question sits elsewhere in the document. During the August comment window, one commenter argued that treating airside international transit as "presence" is indefensible — that a passenger who never clears host-nation immigration, in a disease not transmissible before symptoms appear, poses no community-transmission risk justifying a 21-day prohibition.

CDC's response is the most interesting paragraph in the order. It agrees. It states plainly that Ebola is not transmitted through ordinary airborne exposure and that infected persons generally do not transmit before symptoms. It then declines the change anyway, on operational difficulty: distinguishing transit circumstances at scale would be harder to operationalise given traveller volumes and constraints on personnel.

That is not a scientific refusal. It is a resourcing one — and resourcing objections move when someone shows the alternative is administrable. A boarding-pass sector check is not an exotic ask of a system that already reads itineraries.

This matters to East Africa for geographic rather than epidemiological reasons. Entebbe is a connecting airport, and Uganda Airlines has spent the year making it a better one: Abuja, Lusaka and Harare added, Accra from 27 October, daily Kigali from 18 November. Every addition increases the number of travellers whose only contact with Uganda is ninety minutes in a transit lounge — and who, as the order is written, become inadmissible to the United States for three weeks afterwards.

A hub's entire value is that connecting through it is costless. This order attaches a cost, and attaches it to the transit rather than the destination. Nobody here has noticed, because it does not look like a Ugandan problem. It becomes a Kenyan, Rwandan and Tanzanian one the moment an itinerary routes through EBB.

Then there is the number that should embarrass the industry. On the August order, CDC received three comments. Three — in total, from everywhere.

**WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT**

City hotels and DMCs: audit your own exposure. Any colleague or owner on a non-US passport travelling to a US trade show is caught if their routing touched Entebbe, Juba or Kinshasa inside 21 days, airside included. Your travel desk makes that routing decision without thinking. Make it deliberately.

Bush operators: your American clients are US citizens and not covered. Your European, Gulf, Asian and African clients combining a Uganda gorilla leg with an onward US trip are, and most will not know. A line in pre-departure documentation is cheaper than a denied boarding.

Everyone: file, before 1 October. The docket is CDC-2026-0892 and it is free. Make the transit argument, not the Uganda-is-clean argument, and make it administrable — name the check a port officer could actually run. An agency that concedes your science and refuses on feasibility is telling you what evidence it lacks.

**THE WATCH**

Whether the order replacing this one around 11 October narrows the geography, carves out airside transit, or rolls forward unchanged for a sixth time — and whether the comment count clears single digits.
