## LINKEDIN BIG READ

The most important date in East African hospitality this week isn't in anyone's brochure. It's 12 August — nine days away — and it's the day the United States decides whether travellers from Uganda keep facing entry screening into the autumn.

Here's the setup. In May, an outbreak of Ebola caused by Bundibugyo virus was declared in both DR Congo and Uganda. On 28 July, Uganda's Ministry of Health declared its own outbreak over — 30 days ahead of schedule, with 20 confirmed cases, two deaths, and every transmission chain reconstructed. That is a genuine public-health achievement, and Ugandan operators are right to be proud of it.

But "Uganda is Ebola-free" and "the travel restrictions have lifted" are two different sentences, and the gap between them is exactly where hospitality revenue decisions go wrong.

The restrictions are not keyed to Uganda's caseload. They're keyed to the region's — and specifically to DR Congo, where the same outbreak is not slowing but intensifying. On 30 July we made a falsifiable call: that DRC's confirmed Bundibugyo caseload would pass 3,600 by the WHO situation report around 2 August. It did. WHO's latest Disease Outbreak News logs 3,605 confirmed cases and 1,587 deaths — a case-fatality ratio of 44% — making this the largest Ebola outbreak ever recorded in DRC. We've logged that call as correct, because a brief you can't audit is just opinion with a dateline.

Why should a Congolese caseload govern a Ugandan booking? Because the instruments that actually touch travellers are regional. The US CDC's Section 362 order — the one requiring enhanced entry screening — covers DR Congo, Uganda and South Sudan as a bloc, and it expires on 12 August. The US State Department still holds Uganda at Level 4. Rwanda's 30-day entry restriction on arrivals from DRC is still in force. None of these moved when Kampala declared its all-clear, and the epidemiology gives none of them an obvious reason to move in the next nine days.

So here is the operator playbook for the window, not the commentary.

First, price on the assumption screening holds. Our base case is that CDC renews the order on 12 August with Uganda still named — DRC's trajectory leaves little room to decouple a neighbour before Uganda completes its own 42-day all-clear around 27 August. Quote Ugandan itineraries into September as though a US-bound guest may still face screening. If the order instead drops Uganda, that is upside you announce with a smile — not a promise you made and then had to walk back to an agent.

Second, separate the two sentences in every guest-facing channel. Your reservations team should be able to say, accurately and without drama: the outbreak in Uganda is over; entry and screening rules set by other governments remain in place and are reviewed in mid-August. That is both true and reassuring. "The risk has cleared" is neither.

Third, watch the real dates, not the headlines. 12 August: the CDC order. Around 27 August: Uganda's own 42-day mark, after which the case for keeping it on any regional list weakens sharply. Those two dates, not the next viral post, are when your Uganda advisory language should change.

The wider lesson travels well beyond this outbreak. In a market where a single misread advisory can empty a lodge, the operators who win are the ones who know precisely which instrument governs which guest — and who never confuse a health milestone with a policy change. Nine days out, the disciplined move is patience: hold the line, hold the rate, and let the calendar, not the rumour, tell you when to change your script.
