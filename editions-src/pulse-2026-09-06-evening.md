## LINKEDIN BIG READ

**The safari trip is unbundling at the city end. That is a pricing problem for hotels and a gift to lodges.**

A small item in the South African trade press this week deserves more attention in Nairobi, Kampala, Kigali and Dar than it will get.

**THE SITUATION**

Inbound operators there are describing a consistent split. The client arranges Cape Town themselves, then approaches a specialist for the safari. David Ryan of Rhino Africa says such enquiries have "increased significantly", and puts it plainly: "Cape Town is becoming like a European city. People are feeling much more comfortable booking it themselves." Robyn-Lea Meyer of Giltedge calls it "definitely a consistent pattern", but adds a detail that matters more than the headline — she sees it more often from advisers than from consumers, driven by their own preferred-supplier agreements, loyalty programmes and direct relationships. Jennifer Paterson of Trans Africa Safaris supplies the counterweight: little change in the overall proportion over the past year, and clients who self-book the city component often misread what is included, or book the wrong dates. All three spoke to Tourism Update, published 4 September 2026.

**WHAT EVERYONE IS MISSING**

The trade is reading a channel story. It is a pricing-power story, and it runs in opposite directions along a single itinerary.

What held the bundle together was never loyalty. It was logistics. Meyer names the mechanism precisely: light aircraft schedules, luggage restrictions, private-reserve transfers and lodge availability that changes constantly. An itinerary that looks simple online unravels when those pieces have to align. Where that complexity exists, the intermediary keeps the booking, and price stays illegible to the guest. Where it does not exist — and a city night does not — the booking walks, and the room is then bought against a comp set the guest can see in full.

The consequence is asymmetric. The same behavioural shift commoditises the city bed and quietly strengthens the lodge's hand. That is our inference, not a reported finding, and it is worth stating as such. But if it holds, the timing is unkind. Kenya has 4,922 of its 6,190 pipeline rooms already under construction, on W Hospitality Group's 2026 Africa figures published 10 March 2026 — the highest construction ratio on the continent alongside Ethiopia. A more price-transparent city guest is arriving as the comp set widens.

Then there is Meyer's adviser point, which is the most actionable line in the piece. When the city booking leaves, it does not usually leave for an OTA. It leaves for a hotel the adviser already holds an agreement with. That is a distribution and contracting failure dressed up as a rate problem, and no discount fixes it.

**WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT**

City hotels: audit which consortia, corporate and adviser programmes you are genuinely loaded on and bookable through, not which ones you believe you are on. The 2027 contracting round is being negotiated this quarter, and Magical Kenya Travel Expo runs 6–8 October. Go to win the adviser rather than to defend the rate.

Lodges and camps: resist the instinct to simplify. The friction that makes your product hard to book online is the reason you still own the guest. Fund the operations desk that makes the light-aircraft connection work. That is margin defence, not overhead.

Beach resorts: you sit between the two models. Package the transfer, the boat and the sea-days into something a guest cannot reassemble from separate listings, and the whole stay stays yours.

Everyone: assume the guest has already priced your city room. Paterson's caution is the honest counterweight — this is directional, not yet dominant, and Cape Town is a more mature standalone leisure city than any of ours. Expect the split to reach Nairobi before Kigali, and to arrive later here than there.

**THE WATCH**

Whether East African inbound operators start reporting the same "city on their own, safari with an expert" pattern in their own enquiry mix. And whether any regional city hotel responds by widening consortia distribution rather than by cutting rate. The first is the signal. The second is the test of whether the lesson landed.

Complexity has been quietly subsidising this industry for years. It is worth knowing which half of your itinerary it still protects.
