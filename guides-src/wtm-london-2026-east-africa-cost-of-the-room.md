One year ago today, Isa Kato should have been setting up a table at ExCeL in east London. Instead he was writing from Kampala. Kato, vice-president of the Uganda Tourism Association and managing director of Pristine Tours, was one of 14 Ugandan tour operators who had booked space on the Uganda stand at World Travel Market London 2025. Twelve of them, Kato among them, were refused UK visas. Two went. The rest had already paid close to US$1,000 each for a table, more than US$200 in visa fees, and flights and London accommodation that were not refundable.

"As one of those denied a visa this year, I find myself asking," he wrote on 2 November 2025, "what real value are we gaining from this route if our tourism professionals cannot access or promote this market?"

That question has gone unanswered for a year, and tomorrow it becomes urgent again. World Travel Market London opens at ExCeL on 3 November and runs to 5 November. For the long-haul safari and beach trade, it is the week in which next summer stops being a forecast and becomes a contract: allocations are agreed, release dates are set, cancellation terms are written, and the brochure pages that will carry East Africa into British and European homes in 2027 are allotted or lost. By the organiser's own account of the 2025 edition, 46,500 travel professionals attended from 182 countries, 5,500 buyers came through the doors, an 8 per cent increase, and more than 40,000 meetings were pre-scheduled, a 16 per cent increase. RX Global, which runs the show, attributes over £2.2 billion of business deals to it. Those are organiser figures rather than audited ones, and they should be read as such, but no one in the trade disputes the direction of them.

This year the geography of the floor has changed in a way that should concentrate East African minds. Africa has returned to the main exhibition floor, positioned between Latin America and the Middle East. That is not decoration. It is a statement of the competitive set. The slot being fought over in those three days is a British or German family's single long-haul holiday, and the rivals for it are a Caribbean beach, a Gulf stopover and a Costa Rican rainforest, not the camp down the valley in the Mara.

The stakes are higher for the region this year than last for one concrete reason. British Airways is returning to Tanzania after a fifteen-year absence. BA2163 will fly London Gatwick to Kilimanjaro and on to Zanzibar three times a week, Mondays, Thursdays and Saturdays, from 29 May to 31 October 2027. Those are new seats into the two airports that matter most for the northern circuit and the islands, and they will be packaged, priced and sold by tour operators who are in one building this week. The United Kingdom was already Kenya's largest European source market, with 180,639 arrivals in 2024 according to Tourism Research Institute figures, behind only the United States at 306,501 and Kenya's immediate neighbours Uganda and Tanzania. The UK trade is not a nice-to-have audience for East Africa. It is the second pillar of the long-haul book.

The market those contracts will be written into is weaker than the region's own numbers suggest. UN Tourism's barometer, published on 17 September, recorded about 690 million international arrivals in the first half of 2026, a rise of just 0.4 per cent on the same period a year earlier, and cut the full-year growth forecast to between 1 and 2 per cent from an earlier 3 to 4 per cent. Within that near-flat global picture, Africa grew 4 per cent, Europe 3 per cent, the Americas 2 per cent, while Middle East arrivals fell 22 per cent. Africa is, for now, the fastest-growing region in a stalling world. Virginia Messina, group chief executive of the African Travel and Tourism Association, put it as "Africa's position as the world's fastest-growing tourism region during the first half of this year", and argued that realising the potential "will require a stronger collective voice and closer collaboration between tourism businesses".

Collaboration has a price list, and it is worth setting out plainly because the region rarely does. A workstation on the ATTA stand at WTM London 2026 costs £6,665 for members and £7,330 for non-members, with 20 per cent VAT on top for UK-registered organisations. That buys a desk, four chairs, a branded panel, lockable storage, one exhibitor badge and access to the show's meeting platform. Additional badges cost £225 for the first and £170 for each one after that, and they do not come with more meeting space. ATTA membership itself now starts at £250 excluding VAT, under a five-tier structure that took effect on 1 October. Add return flights from Nairobi, Entebbe, Dar es Salaam or Kigali, five nights in London in November, and the visa fee, and a single small operator's presence at this show runs comfortably past the equivalent of a month's revenue from a mid-season lodge.

Set that beside what others spend. Nepal's tourism board put out a tender in September with a ceiling of US$151,000 to design, build and remove its WTM stand for itself and 35 co-exhibitors. China's pavilion is close to 22 per cent larger than last year. Italy is preparing more than 2,000 square metres. East Africa's presence on the ATTA stand this year, by the association's own co-exhibitor list, amounts to eight businesses: AirKenya Group, Heritage Hotels, Green Inspirations DMC, Beach and Safari Holidays, Gosheni Safaris, Karibu Camps and Lodges, Private Safaris and Sense of Africa East Africa, with Serena Hotels among the operators newly signed for the show. Eight firms and a hotel group, sharing a stand, against pavilions measured in thousands of square metres.

There is a reasonable argument that a shared stand is the efficient choice for small businesses, and that buyers come for the diary rather than the carpet. The problem is not the size of the stand. It is that nobody in the region can say what the money buys. No East African tourism board publishes the cost of its WTM participation alongside the qualified leads, the contracts signed, or the room nights and seats that followed. This is not a uniquely East African failing, and it is beginning to be challenged elsewhere on the continent. On 29 September, South Africa's parliamentary portfolio committee on tourism asked its own agencies for economic impact findings and reporting on contracts, investment and jobs arising from Africa's Travel Indaba and Meetings Africa. Tourism Ireland requires businesses to complete a post-event evaluation before they are eligible for future events. A survey is not an audit, and a committee request is not a published return, but both are a step past the current regional position, which is to treat attendance as self-evidently worthwhile and to count footfall as outcome.

The accountability gap and the visa gate are the same problem seen from two ends. Both are about access that is bought but not secured. Thomas Ddumba, a solicitor of the Senior Courts of England and Wales, wrote in Kampala's Daily Monitor that nearly half of Ugandan visitor visa applications were refused in 2025, against about 30 per cent in 2020, citing UK official figures. That specific rate is a commentary claim rather than a figure this publication has verified against the Home Office data tables, and it should be carried with that caveat. What is firsthand and documented is Kato's count: 12 refusals out of 14 applicants, from a delegation that had already paid.

The encouraging part is that the arithmetic appears to change when the state turns up. In the last week of October, the Uganda Tourism Board ran a week-long UK roadshow with the Uganda High Commission in London, covering Glasgow, Manchester and Birmingham. Twelve Ugandan tour operators travelled. In Glasgow alone, a five-hour evening with a destination presentation by Dorcas Namajja, UTB's senior marketing officer, drew 23 travel trade representatives from Glasgow and Edinburgh, and the roadshow produced more than 60 business-to-business meetings. One agent's reaction, recorded by Nile Post, is the whole case for being physically present: "I had never thought about Uganda. After tonight, I'm coming to Uganda next year." A representative of Allan Water Travel said a group of eight would travel.

Twelve operators refused for a trade fair in November 2025; twelve operators in Scotland and England in October 2026, under a high commission's wing. The difference is not luck. It is institutional sponsorship, applied early, with a diplomatic mission's letterhead behind the application. That is a tool East African boards already possess and deploy unevenly, and it costs a fraction of a larger stand.

Which brings the argument to what should actually happen inside the building this week. The temptation at a trade fair is to sell the destination: the wildebeest, the gorillas, the white sand. The destination is not in doubt. What is in doubt is the terms, and the terms are where 2027 margin lives. For the new Gatwick seats in particular, the question an operator should put to a UK buyer is not whether they will feature Tanzania, but on what allocation, with what release date, in which currency, at what commission, and with whose money at risk if the flight sells slowly in a year the global forecast has already been halved. This publication has argued before that added seats arrive as rate dilution when allocation is handed over without a release date that the seller can live with, and a May to October season on a thrice-weekly service gives a buyer a great deal of room to hold inventory it never intends to sell.

There is one further timing oddity worth noting. The WTM Global Travel Report, the show's own read on where demand is going, is unveiled on the opening morning of 3 November and repeated on the final day. Rob Ransom, Booking Holdings' chief strategy officer, is on the programme in the same week his company reports its third quarter, and Andrew Flintham, managing director of British Airways Holidays, is also speaking. The intelligence, in other words, arrives after a good many East African operators have already filed their 2027 rates. Those who have not yet committed have three days of unusually cheap information in front of them.

Chris Carter-Chapman, WTM London's event director, has framed this year's conference theme as "Resilience, Rebound and Reinvention: the next era of global travel." For East Africa the resilience question is narrower and more awkward than the slogan suggests. The region is the growth story in a flat market, with new British seats into Tanzania and a trade fair in its largest European market opening in the morning. It still cannot say what that market costs it to attend, what it earns there, or how many of the people it paid to send will be allowed through the door. Kato's question from a year ago remains the right standard to measure against, and it is a question for Nairobi, Kampala, Dar es Salaam and Kigali at least as much as for London.
