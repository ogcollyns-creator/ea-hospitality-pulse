Muyuni Beach had a good week. On 6 November, Iberostar opened a 358 room resort on the sand there, its first property in Tanzania, the latest and largest piece of evidence that the archipelago's long run of record arrivals has convinced European capital to build rather than contract. Two days later, every other hotel on Unguja and Pemba began reading that it had until 15 December to install closed circuit television.

The reports were specific in a way that government announcements in the region rarely are. High definition cameras, a minimum of 1080p at fifteen frames a second, positioned at vehicle access gates, beachfront boundaries, reception foyers and communal pathways. Infrared capable of identifying a face or a number plate at thirty metres. A dedicated network video recorder held on site, with cloud only recording restricted, retaining at least thirty calendar days of continuous footage. Two hours of uninterruptible power supply. Weatherproof housings rated IP66 or IP67 for the units facing the sea. Cameras forbidden inside guest bedrooms, en suite bathrooms, spa treatment rooms, enclosed villa balconies and walled plunge pools, with a breach of that prohibition carrying permanent revocation of the tourism operating licence rather than a fine. Compliance audits beginning on 16 December, the day after the deadline.

An operator reading that list on a Monday morning in November would reasonably start phoning suppliers. The difficulty is that the list cannot be traced to anything. The two accounts carrying the technical detail, published on 9 November by Travel And Tour World and by the site Nomad Lawyer, attribute the requirement jointly to the Revolutionary Government of Zanzibar, the Ministry of Tourism and Heritage and the Zanzibar Commission for Tourism. Neither cites a circular number, a gazette notice, a statutory instrument or a regulation. Neither quotes a named official. One of the two concedes in its own text that it could not verify the ministry website it links to. A third account, published a day earlier by Tanzania Insight, describes something different again: cameras across every tourist hotel and lodge on both islands, no deadline, no specification, no penalties, and footage "linked to central government monitoring stations for real time oversight".

That last discrepancy is not a detail. It is the whole commercial question. A system that records to a network video recorder in the back office, which a security manager can access and the police can extract from on a warrant, is a known quantity: cameras, cabling, a recorder, a battery, a maintenance contract. A system that streams continuously to a government monitoring centre is a different product with different bandwidth, different network hardware, a permanent external dependency and an entirely different legal character, because the hotel is then not holding footage of its guests, it is transmitting it. The three published accounts of the same reported directive describe both architectures. No operator can procure against that.

The temptation is to dismiss the whole thing as trade press noise, and that would be the expensive mistake. Zanzibar regulates its hospitality industry by directive, and its directives bind. In March 2025 the Commission for Tourism announced a mandatory waste management programme under the Greener Zanzibar banner, effective from 1 July that year, requiring every tourism firm to train staff in sorting and disposal, separate and store organic and recyclable streams on site, and contract a licensed collector. It was communicated, by the Commission's own account, through local leadership, media outreach and quarterly stakeholder meetings. Not through a gazette. Arif Abbas Manji, the Commission's executive secretary, explained the logic in a sentence that is worth any operator's attention this month. "Sustainable practices have been optional for a very long time," he said. The programme was "instrumental in ensuring sustainable practices are mandatory to ensure continuity in the tourism sector in Zanzibar".

That is an administration which states plainly that its method is converting the optional into the obligatory, and which does so through announcement rather than instrument. The July 2024 registration regime for non hotel accommodation followed the same pattern: unveiled at a stakeholders' meeting by Mudrick Ramadhani Soraga, then the tourism minister, requiring registration with the Commission followed by inspection and licence, at Sh150,000 in Mjini Magharibi and Sh250,000 elsewhere, with non compliance carrying fines up to five thousand dollars or imprisonment of between three months and five years. An owner who waited for a published regulation before registering would have been waiting while inspectors arrived.

So the honest position for a Zanzibar hotelier is neither that the CCTV requirement is real nor that it is invented. It is that a deadline is circulating, that the body it is attributed to has a demonstrated habit of making announced requirements stick, and that nobody outside the trade press can say what the requirement actually is. Five weeks is not enough time to resolve that by waiting.

The timing compounds it. December capital expenditure on this coast is funded out of December deposits, which is to say out of the cash that pays for the season the cameras are supposedly protecting. Zanzibar's arrivals through the end of October 2025 reached 743,605, the highest figure in the islands' history according to the Office of the Chief Government Statistician, and the records have continued: 124,481 in August 2026 alone, with British visitors up 21.2 per cent year on year and Italian arrivals more than doubling from July's 8,678. That is a destination with pricing power on paper. It is also a destination where the levy architecture has been tightening for a year, with 142 hotels now linked to the government's digital collection system and service development levy receipts of Sh18.08 billion in the 2024/25 financial year. A capital requirement arriving unpriced, five weeks before the peak, into a cost base already absorbing a digitised levy and a new wage order, is not a rounding error.

The deeper problem is not the money. It is what thirty days of retained footage does to a hotel's legal position.

A property holding thirty calendar days of continuous, identifiable video of its guests, at gates, at reception and along the pathways between the two, is a data controller handling personal data at scale, whatever the directive calls it. The reported requirement is careful about who may look at that footage. It says access is restricted to vetted security managers and that extraction requires officers of the police or the Ministry of Health acting under a formal warrant. It says nothing, in any of the three published versions, about who is liable when the recorder is stolen, when a staff member photographs a monitor, or when a guest from Munich writes in February asking what was recorded of her children in January and who has seen it.

Tanzania's Personal Data Protection Act No. 11 of 2022 has been operational since 1 May 2023 and is not a light instrument. It establishes a Personal Data Protection Commission with power to register collectors and processors and to enter their premises to check whether security requirements are met. Its penalty notices run to TZS 100 million. Unlawful disclosure by an individual carries between TZS 100,000 and TZS 20 million, imprisonment of up to ten years, or both. For a company the range runs from TZS 1 million to TZS 5 billion. Any operator being told to accumulate identifiable footage should know those numbers before the first camera goes up.

Then there is the jurisdictional seam, and it is genuinely unresolved. Legal commentary on the 2022 Act holds that while it extends to Zanzibar, it does so only in respect of union matters. Tourism licensing and internal security are not union matters. Zanzibar has been working towards its own data protection law since at least 2023, when the Law Reforms Commission's baseline survey found a need for one and stakeholders were pressing for it. "So that we come up with the best law in data protection," Mussa Kombo of the Commission said then, framing the objective as protecting "citizens' information while enhancing citizens' right to privacy and personal security". As far as can be established, that law has not arrived. Reporting at the time described the union Act as simply applying in the isles, which is a different reading. Neither position has been tested on a tourism matter.

A hotel on Nungwi beach is therefore being asked, on the strength of an untraceable directive, to become the custodian of a category of data whose governing law on the islands is contested, under a regulator whose jurisdiction over the activity is unclear, for a retention period it did not choose, for the benefit of agencies it cannot refuse. The guests whose faces fill that archive are increasingly European, from markets where the handling of recorded images of identifiable people, children above all, is a settled expectation rather than an emerging one. The first subject access request to reach a Zanzibar resort will not be answered by citing a report in the trade press.

None of this is an argument against cameras. Perimeter surveillance at a beachfront property is ordinary good practice, most larger resorts on the islands already have it, and the stated rationale, deterring opportunistic property crime and shortening emergency response, is unobjectionable on its own terms. The objection is to procuring a security system, a data retention obligation and a licence risk from a specification that no issuing authority has acknowledged.

What that means in practice is narrow and worth doing this week. Write to the Commission for Tourism and ask for the instrument by number and date, and keep the reply or the absence of one. Ask the single question the published accounts contradict each other on: does footage remain on the property, or does it leave it, and if it leaves, to whom and over whose connection. Ask what the retention period is, in writing, because retention is the liability and thirty days of identifiable video is a much larger exposure than seven. Ask what the lawful basis is for recording guests and what notice the hotel is expected to display. Do not buy to a frame rate and an ingress rating published by an aggregator, because if a real specification follows it may not match, and equipment bought to the wrong standard in November is not a compliance defence in December. Industry bodies exist for precisely this: a single written query from the Zanzibar Association of Tourism Investors would settle in a day what three hundred individual owners cannot settle at all.

The structural point reaches well beyond one archipelago and one set of cameras. Across East Africa the operative compliance calendar is increasingly set not by gazettes but by whichever outlet publishes a requirement first, in a specification detailed enough to be actionable and sourced too thinly to be checkable. Operators then spend real capital against it, because the cost of being wrong about a regulator's intentions is higher than the cost of the equipment. That asymmetry is the thing to notice. It is not a surveillance story. It is a story about who gets to set a hotel's capital expenditure, and about how little it now takes to do so.
