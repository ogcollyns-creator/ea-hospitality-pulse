Christopher Nassetta has been in the hotel business for more than four decades, and on Wednesday afternoon in New York he told a room full of travel executives that he had never seen a stretch quite like the one his owners have just lived through.

"It's really abnormal in my experience of 40-plus years of doing this, where you would have essentially, other than a sugar high, a really difficult 10 years," Hilton's president and chief executive said on the Skift Global Forum stage on 23 September, running through the sequence: the long convalescence after 2008, growth he called "really anemic" in 2017 and 2018, a pandemic that removed more than 90 per cent of revenue, a stimulus-fuelled rebound, then several years of thin growth against stubborn inflation.

What made the remarks worth reading from Nairobi was not the diagnosis. It was the number attached to the cure. Hilton's answer to a decade of squeezed owner returns is a cost review it calls Project RISE, and on the company's second-quarter earnings call in July Nassetta put its value to owners at "somewhere between 75 and 100 basis points in margin." The programme, launched in 2025 and rolled out through this year, sits alongside a cut in loyalty fees and a scheme that discounts fees for hotels delivering strong guest scores. The three cost lines it goes after are labour, energy and insurance. The next phase, Nassetta said in New York, leans on artificial intelligence to take out distribution and operating cost.

Read that as a shift in what a global brand is selling. For thirty years the pitch to an owner in an emerging market was demand: sign with us and the rooms fill. The pitch now increasingly includes the expense side of the profit and loss account, because that is where owners have been losing. And it is arriving in East Africa at precisely the moment the region's owners are signing the kind of contract that makes the expense side entirely theirs.

Hilton signed 29 deals across 15 African countries in 2025 and says it remains on track to open more than 100 hotels on the continent, over half of them in sub-Saharan Africa, taking its African portfolio past 180 properties trading and in development. "Sub-Saharan Africa remains a strategically important growth region for Hilton," Carlos Khneisser, the group's chief development officer for the Middle East and Africa, said when the signings were announced in the spring. East Africa's share of that includes the AVA Hotel Nairobi, a 91-room property in Lavington that brings the Tapestry Collection to Kenya for the first time, and Canopy by Hilton Zanzibar The Burj.

The structural change underneath those signings is the one that deserves an owner's attention. For years the African pipeline ran on management agreements, where the chain takes over the operation: Trevor Ward of W Hospitality Group has put it at roughly 81 per cent of the continent's 2024 pipeline. That is now moving. The firm's January 2026 count, reported in March, found 146 franchise agreements in the African development pipeline, 35 per cent more than a year earlier and 22 per cent of the total, up from 18.7 per cent.

Ward's own description of the difference is the whole argument in a sentence. Under a franchise, he said, "the chain provides the brand and supports revenue generation, but day-to-day operations remain the owner's responsibility."

So when a brand promises 75 to 100 basis points of margin, an owner on a franchise deal is not being offered a share of someone else's operating improvement. They are being told that their own costs will fall because of programmes the brand runs. That claim is testable, line by line, and in East Africa it does not survive contact with two of the three lines.

Start with energy. Kenya's September pass-through charge was set by gazette notice on 18 September at Ksh4.16 for every kilowatt hour consumed, made up of a Ksh3.00 fuel energy cost charge covering thermal generation at Kipevu III and Rabai, roughly Ksh1.14 of foreign exchange fluctuation adjustment tied to some Ksh1.32 billion of currency cost, and a fraction of a cent for water resource management. A hotel in Westlands pays that because the regulator published it, and it pays the forex component because the shilling moved. There is no global procurement contract, no group energy desk and no AI scheduling tool that negotiates a gazette notice. The lever the brand can genuinely pull, consumption, is the one Kenyan operators have already been pulling for three years, and the route off the grid narrowed this month when the same regulator halved the credit paid for power that a property exports back into it.

Labour is worse, because it is set by law and it has just moved. Tanzania's Labour Institutions (Minimum Wage for Private Sector) Order, published as Government Notice 605A in October last year, took effect on 1 January and rewrote the floor for accommodation. Five and four-star hotels: TZS 375,000 a month. Three-star: TZS 225,000. One and two-star hotels, guesthouses, lodgings, bars and restaurants: TZS 195,000. Tour guides: TZS 320,000. A hotel owner in Dar es Salaam or on the Zanzibar coast did not negotiate those numbers with a brand, a union or a supplier. They were gazetted.

The productivity half of the labour promise is real, and it is the part East African owners should look at most sceptically, because of where it is measured. HotelData's half-year report, published on 17 September, found United States full-service hotels cut hours per occupied room by 3.1 per cent year on year and select-service hotels by 3.5 per cent, with room attendants at select-service properties taking 22.82 minutes a room against 24.01 a year earlier, while hourly housekeeping wages rose between 2.9 and 3.3 per cent. That is a genuine gain and a well-instrumented one. It was also produced inside American select-service labour models, on stock that looks nothing like a Zanzibar beach resort with a full kitchen brigade, or a Mara camp that houses and feeds its staff on site because there is nowhere else for them to live.

Insurance is the line where global scale most plausibly does travel, since placement is an international market and a brand-wide programme can price risk across hundreds of properties. It is also the hardest line to verify from outside a signed agreement, and no owner should accept it as a rounding assumption. The right response to a margin claim built partly on insurance is to ask for the placement: which carrier, which programme, which currency, and what an East African property's loss history actually does to its share of it.

The revenue half of the brands' case has held up better, and it is getting more concentrated. Anthony Capuano, Marriott's chief executive, told a Bank of America conference on 9 September that "the biggest impact you'll see on our industry is the distribution landscape," and described feeding Google and OpenAI large volumes of portfolio content so that Marriott hotels "show up in a really both authentic but prominent way." Nassetta made the same argument from Hilton's side in New York, saying the company intends to be present in every channel a customer books through, with the scale to stay in the relationship: a quarter of United States quality supply and a loyalty programme he put at 300 million members.

For a city hotel in Nairobi or Kigali carrying a global flag, that is worth real money and should not be discounted. A Hilton Honors member choosing Lavington over an unbranded competitor is exactly the mechanism owners pay for. But it decays fast as you move away from the transient business traveller. The demand that fills a Zanzibar resort in the European winter, or a Serengeti camp in the migration, is contracted months in advance through French, German, Italian and British tour operators, and no loyalty database substitutes for a place in a long-haul programme. An owner signing a franchise this quarter is buying strong visibility in the channels that matter least to their peak season.

There is also a cost in that promise that nobody in New York would allocate. On the same day Nassetta spoke, Elena Avila, who runs travel distribution at Amadeus, and Steve Singh of Spotnana were on another Skift stage discussing who pays for the compute, search load, security and data governance that agentic AI booking requires. Neither would say. Avila's warning was that the revenue is not automatic: "I think we shouldn't just be complacent and think that revenue will simply come." An unallocated cost in a franchised system has a default home, and it is the owner's profit and loss account, arriving as a line on a monthly fee statement.

Which is why the most interesting counter-structure in African hospitality right now is not a brand at all. In June last year Aleph Hospitality took over management of 26 Onomo hotels across 14 African countries, a deal its executive director Neil George described as "the largest transition from management to franchising ever in the continent." George was pointed about what Aleph sells against: "It is not remote-controlled management, which we have an allergy for." The firm now operates more than 50 hotels in 23 countries with another 30 in development, is opening offices in Nairobi, Cape Town, Casablanca and Abidjan, and targets 100 hotels by the end of 2029. Its founder, Bani Haddad, reads the growth as a verdict on structure: "Our continued growth reflects the strength of our business model and the increasing demand from hotel owners for an experienced independent operator in the region." Aleph is also the operator running Kenya's first Tapestry Collection hotel under the Hilton flag.

That is the shape of the choice now available to an East African owner, and it is better than the one available five years ago. Licence the brand for what it demonstrably delivers, distribution and recognition, and buy operating capability from someone whose own margin depends on East African costs rather than American ones.

Three things belong in the negotiation before any margin promise is paid for. Ask the brand to name, for your country, the counterparty behind each cost programme it is claiming: the supplier, the contract, the currency. Ask for a cap and a stated basis for technology and distribution surcharges, because the industry's tech bill is rising and its own executives will not say who carries it. And ask for the brand's delivered share of room nights at comparable properties in your market rather than the global figure, because the global figure is mostly America.

The offer on the table has genuinely improved. A basis point, though, is only a basis point where the brand can reach the cost. In Nairobi the price of a kilowatt hour was decided by a notice dated 18 September. In Dar es Salaam the wage floor was decided by a government notice last October. Neither was negotiable, neither appears in a franchise agreement, and between them they move more of an East African hotel's margin than any programme designed in Virginia.
