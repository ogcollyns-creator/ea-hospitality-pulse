The announcement ran to a few hundred words and never used the phrase that mattered. On 25 November, from Seattle, Expedia Group said it had agreed to acquire Kiwi.com, the Czech flight platform founded in 2012, and described it in the blandest terms available: a company that "uses proprietary technology to help travelers search and book air travel". Ariane Gorin, Expedia Group's chief executive, said she was "delighted to welcome Kiwi.com to Expedia Group" and that "Oliver and team have built an innovative flights platform that already serves millions of travelers". Oliver Dlouhý, Kiwi.com's co-founder and chief executive, called it "a deeply meaningful moment for me" after fourteen years. The deal is expected to close in the first half of 2027, subject to customary closing conditions. No price was disclosed, and Expedia declined to give one when Skift asked.

Three phrases are absent from that announcement. Virtual interlining. Self-transfer. The Kiwi.com Guarantee. They are the only three things about Kiwi.com that an East African hotelier needs to understand, and the silence around them is the most interesting part of the document.

What Kiwi.com built, and what Expedia is paying for, is the ability to sell a journey that no single party has promised to complete. Skift describes virtual interlining as combining flights from airlines without commercial agreements into single itineraries: the traveller buys what looks like one trip, London to Zanzibar by way of somewhere, and receives two or three separately ticketed legs from carriers that do not sell connections on one another's planes. The itinerary exists on the screen. It does not exist in any contract. When the first leg runs late and the traveller watches the second one push back from the gate, Skift put the position plainly in a follow-up piece on 26 November: no airline is obliged to rebook them.

That is not a loophole. It is the product. And it is the product East Africa is sold through, on this publication's reading of how the region is actually reached, because East Africa is not a destination anybody arrives at directly. The itineraries that deliver a guest to a camp in the Mara, a lodge on the Nile or a beach property at Paje are assembled out of connections, with a light aircraft on the last leg more often than not. Safarilink, based at Wilson Airport in Nairobi, flies eight Cessna 208B Caravans listed at twelve passengers each. A party of four that misses the morning departure out of Wilson is not waiting two hours for the next wide-body. It is losing a day of a four-night safari, and somebody is going to pay for that day.

The question of who has just become a good deal sharper, because Europe has written the answer into law.

Regulation (EU) 2026/2202, adopted on 16 September 2026 and published in the Official Journal on 2 October, rewrites Regulation 261/2004, the instrument that governs what a European passenger is owed when a flight goes wrong. Read the new text carefully and the striking thing is how carefully it fences the protection off. The new Article 6b, inserted to deal with exactly the scenario above, is titled "Missed connecting flight during journeys performed pursuant to a single air transport contract". A single air transport contract. Within that fence the passenger does well: the operating carrier of the disrupted earlier flight must reroute them, and if it cannot do so "within five hours from the time of departure indicated on the passenger's ticket of the missed connecting flight", reimbursement follows. A "journey", as the amended Article 2 defines it, is "a flight or connecting flights transporting the passenger from the initial point of departure", with outbound and return flights being separate journeys.

Outside the fence, the law's answer to the self-transfer traveller is not protection. It is a warning label. The replaced Article 14(3) obliges the seller, whether an airline or an intermediary, to inform the passenger before purchase that the tickets are covered by separate air transport contracts, and that the rights to compensation, reimbursement, rerouting and assistance "do not apply under the separate air transport contract". An intermediary, under the amended companion regulation, is any person "other than an air carrier" acting for a passenger or a carrier in concluding a transport contract. On that definition Expedia Group is an intermediary, and so is every tour operator and travel agency that resells its supply.

So Europe's considered position, arrived at through conciliation in June and votes in July, is that the unprotected connection is lawful, that the buyer must be told it is unprotected, and that nobody is then responsible for it. The disclosure is the remedy. It is not even a remedy yet: the date from which the new rules apply is reported to fall in the second half of 2027, and that date is the one provision of the regulation this publication was unable to read for itself. Which raises the question nobody in the trade press has asked this week: if the law has just confirmed that the seller of a self-transfer itinerary owes the passenger nothing but a warning, what exactly is Expedia buying?

The honest answer is that it is buying the only part of the business that ever cost money. Kiwi.com's distinctive asset was never the search. Plenty of companies search. Its distinctive asset was that it stood behind the itineraries it stitched together when the law did not: it built its Guarantee, priced the disruption risk, and ran the operations behind it. Rerouting a stranded traveller on a ticket no carrier recognises is slow, manual and expensive, and it is why Skift's September analysis found Skyscanner, which simply refers travellers to booking partners and carries none of this, substantially more profitable.

Here is where the numbers stop being flattering. Kiwi.com's revenue fell from €284m in 2023 to €227m in 2025, a drop of about a fifth, which the company attributed to lower ticket prices. Over the same stretch operating profit rose fivefold, to roughly €15m. It got there by cutting: personnel costs down 31 per cent, and customer support spending down 32 per cent. Bookings, meanwhile, were up more than 50 per cent year on year in 2026, according to the Czech publication E15. More itineraries, each one carrying an unpriced possibility of disruption, serviced by a support function a third smaller than it was.

That is the company Expedia has agreed to buy, and it is the shape of the risk that now matters to an operator in Arusha or Entebbe. A platform that has demonstrated it can make virtual interlining profitable by thinning the thing that catches people when it fails has not solved the problem. It has relocated it.

Where it relocates to is the part the release gestures at without describing. Gorin said the combination would "strengthen Brand Expedia and our B2B business' position in air", and the release says the deal will "unlock growth opportunities for both Expedia Group's consumer brands and B2B partners". Expedia's B2B arm is the quieter half of the company and the faster-growing one: third-quarter revenue up 23 per cent to $1.71bn, though adjusted EBITDA margin slipped 134 basis points to 27.5 per cent. It is the engine behind other people's booking screens.

What the release does not say, and what nobody should assert on its behalf, is that self-transfer itineraries will be pushed into those B2B feeds. Expedia has committed to nothing of the kind, and Kiwi.com is to keep operating as a standalone brand. But the strategic logic of putting differentiated air supply into a channel that resells to intermediaries is not obscure, and the consequence, if it happens, is specific: the itinerary type stops being something a price-sensitive traveller deliberately selects and becomes something that arrives in a package, bought by a guest who never saw the word "self-transfer" and sold by an agent who may not have looked. The warning label the new European regulation requires is served to the person at the point of purchase. The person at the far end of the itinerary, holding a reservation, is not in the transaction at all.

East African operators have an unusually vivid reason to think about this right now. On Friday 27 November, Kenya Airways told passengers that Nairobi airspace would be "temporarily closed on 27 November 2026, from 12:00 HRS to 13:20 HRS", and again on 4 and 12 December from 10:30 to 12:30, "to facilitate scheduled national military activities". The airline said it was "working closely with the relevant authorities to minimise disruption" and asked passengers to check their flight status before travelling to the airport. The advisory for the first closure went out the same day it took effect. For a guest on one contract, that is the airline's problem. For a guest on three, it is nobody's, until the guest reaches the front desk.

That desk is the real point of this piece. The bed-night lost to a broken self-transfer is not an aviation cost that happens to touch hospitality. It is a hospitality cost created by an aviation product, and the chain of remedy runs the wrong way. The guest's claim against the carriers is worth very little, and the regulation adopted in September confirms that rather than changing it. The lodge's claim is against the guest, under a cancellation policy the lodge wrote itself. So the argument happens at check-in, between the only two parties in the whole arrangement who did nothing wrong, while the companies that assembled the itinerary are not present and owe nobody anything.

Operators can do more about this than they currently do, and almost none of it requires anyone's permission. The arrival itinerary is a question that can be asked at the point of booking and very rarely is: how many tickets, and is the final leg on the same one. A property that knows the answer can write a different arrival-day clause for a self-transfer guest than for a through-ticketed one, hold the first night on a different basis, and stop discovering the problem at six in the evening when the transfer driver has been at the airstrip since noon. The operators who already do this are mostly the ones who got burned.

Meanwhile the region is asking for more of exactly the legs that will be sold this way. At the launch of the Pearl of Africa Tourism Expo in Kampala on 26 November, Dr James Musinguzi, executive director of the Uganda Wildlife Authority, pressed for domestic air service to Kidepo and Kisoro, and for private money in beds: "It's not all about wildlife," he said. "Can we have more accommodation?" Juliana Kagwa, the Uganda Tourism Board's chief executive, said the joint East African expo next May would take up visas, seamless travel and more domestic air routes. Tom Butime, the tourism minister, called the 2027 football "an opportunity that goes far beyond football".

They are right to want the connectivity. It is worth noticing what kind of connectivity the market is currently best at supplying, and who is holding the bill when it does not connect.
