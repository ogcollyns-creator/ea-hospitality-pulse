Google's AI Mode will now book the room. Nothing in it has a field for the licence Kenya requires.

By Onyango George (OG)

Fifty-two per cent of American travellers planned a trip with AI this year. About two per cent booked through one. Phocuswright's director of research called the gap "the number to watch", and three days of conference panels agreed on why it exists: nobody has settled who is accountable when an agentic booking goes wrong. The industry's three answers to that question are loyalty scale, merchant of record, and refusal. An East African lodge has access to none of them.

Alicia Schmid put two numbers on a screen in Fort Lauderdale last week and let the room sit with them. Fifty-two per cent of American travellers had used artificial intelligence to plan a trip in the previous twelve months. About two per cent had booked through an AI platform, rising to roughly eight per cent if you counted Google's AI Mode. Schmid, Phocuswright's director of research, then added the figure that gives the first two their meaning: forty-three per cent of travellers who are familiar with AI say they would book inside an AI platform. The distance between forty-three and two, she told delegates at the Signia by Hilton Diplomat Beach Resort, is "the number to watch."

It is worth being precise about what that gap is not. It is not a capability gap. Steve Domin of Duffel, which sells the plumbing that turns an airline offer into a ticket, told the same conference that the booking itself goes through smoothly, and that the genuinely hard engineering sits after the booking, in changes and cancellations. Panellists across the three days converged on the same diagnosis: the technology to complete an agentic booking exists, and what is holding back volume is the unresolved question of who is accountable when something goes wrong. Phocuswright's companion survey of around 150 travel companies found forty-five per cent scaling or already scaled on agentic AI, up from twenty-eight per cent in 2025. The supply side is building. The demand side is hovering with its card in its hand.

Three answers to the accountability question surfaced last week, and they are worth separating, because East African operators can reach exactly none of them.

The first is loyalty scale. Chris Wilroy, Hilton's executive vice president and chief commercial officer, described the group's Hilton Honors programme and its 270 million members as a trust signal to the AI systems now recommending hotels. The argument is that an agent choosing between properties, and a traveller deciding whether to let it, both read membership at that scale as a guarantee of recourse. Hilton's other two bets are a technology platform it controls and room data detailed enough for an agent to match against a guest's stated needs.

The second answer is the merchant of record, and it is the one that has just been written into live infrastructure. Google's hotel booking inside AI Mode has launched in the United States following a pilot, with inventory from Expedia, Booking.com, Hilton, Hotels.com, Choice, IHG, Priceline, Marriott, Wyndham and Trip.com. James Byers, a group product manager for Google Search, set out the mechanics plainly: the traveller pays with Google Pay, and the hotel or the online travel agency is the merchant of record and handles customer service after the booking. Byers said that arrangement "could change in the future". Partners receive only the data relevant to the booking, not the preceding searches or the conversation. Results are organic and ranked as in traditional search, with no paid placements, though Byers allowed that advertising could appear eventually: "It's early right now." Flight booking is not live. Google's Universal Commerce Protocol is the intended common layer, and Byers said that "as we scale, we will rely on UCP".

The third answer is refusal. Jane Gasparian Lee, head of B2B and international marketing at United Airlines, confirmed that United currently blocks AI agents from booking directly into its inventory. She said the airline welcomes AI for research and inspiration but wants a human involved at the point of purchase while it works out how to protect customer data. Brett Burgess of ATPCO read the decision as being about verifying who the buyer actually is.

Capital is already moving to secure the accountable position rather than the recommending one. Steve Hafner, who co-founded Kayak, described an AI concierge called Lola, built by a team of fourteen people and carrying a hundred million dollar commitment from Booking Holdings, live for thirty days and selling restaurants, concerts, sport and travel through a membership model on the back of direct partnerships with GetYourGuide, Viator, Hertz, CLEAR and Blade. Hafner's reading of the wider field was blunt: no AI agent has yet turned discovery into bookings. Gonzalo García Estebarena, chief executive of Despegar, said his company's own agent already accounts for eight per cent of sales. Alfonso Paredes, Expedia Group's president of B2B and chief commercial officer, offered the formulation the week will probably be remembered by, calling agentic booking overhyped in the short term and underhyped in the long term.

Filip Filipov, chief executive of OAG, supplied the sentence that organises all three. Whoever handles the transaction, he argued, holds the real power, not the AI agent doing the recommending. That is the proposition East Africa needs to examine, because the region's product has no natural transaction holder.

A hotel room in Frankfurt is one supplier selling one thing, and the merchant-of-record rule resolves cleanly: Marriott or Booking.com takes the money and answers the phone. A Kenyan safari is not that. It is a lodge, a conservancy fee, a park entry fee, a light aircraft with a fifteen kilogram soft bag limit, a road transfer, sometimes a gorilla or chimpanzee permit in a neighbouring country with its own calendar, and a rate that changes three times a year. No single party in that chain is positioned to be merchant of record for the whole of it, and the lodge at the end of it is the party least positioned of all. It controls perhaps a third of the invoice and none of the connecting logistics on which the guest's satisfaction actually turns. When an agentic booking of an East African itinerary goes wrong, the thing that went wrong is usually not the room.

So the merchant of record for the region's signature product is, and will remain, the tour operator or the online travel agency. That is not a new dependency. What is new is that it is being hard-coded. The OTA era put an intermediary between the lodge and the guest and charged rent for the position. The agentic era is now writing that intermediary into the settlement layer as the party who is accountable, which is a materially stronger claim on the relationship than a commission ever was. An operator who assumed the agent would disintermediate the OTA has the direction of travel backwards.

There is a second collision, and Kenya has already walked into it. On 20 July the Tourism Regulatory Authority issued an advisory directing every tourism enterprise, and it named online travel platforms explicitly alongside hotels, tour operators, travel agents and destination management companies, to verify a counterparty's licensing status before entering a commercial agreement or making a payment. No licensed enterprise, the Authority said, should pay commissions, referral fees or any other remuneration to a party required to hold a licence under the Tourism Act unless that party holds a valid one. Norbert Talam, the TRA's director-general, pointed stakeholders at the Authority's online verification platform. The Kenya Association of Travel Agents, writing in August, noted that the 2026 rules also restrict digital platforms from listing unlicensed enterprises and require licensed enterprises advertising online to display licence identification details.

Read those two regimes against each other. Kenya's answer to "who is responsible when it goes wrong" is a licence, held by a named enterprise, verifiable in a state register, priced in the fee schedule at KSh21,500 a year for a Kenyan tour operator and around KSh194,334 for a foreign one. Google's answer is a merchant of record, nominated at the point of sale, with post-booking service attached. Nothing in last week's disclosures connects them. No source described any mechanism by which an agentic booking flow verifies a supplier's regulatory licence, and the only allocation of responsibility anyone put on the record was the merchant-of-record line. Kenya has built a trust infrastructure that the trust infrastructure being built in Fort Lauderdale cannot see, and has written an obligation on online platforms that assumes a platform knows whose licence it is checking.

Which leaves the one lever East African operators do hold, and it is unglamorous. Marco Di Cesare of Lumina Frontier ran ninety-nine queries at ChatGPT asking about hotel prices. Ninety-five came back with a price. Only eighteen specified which night the price was for. Booking sites or Google Hotels were cited in eighty of the answers, and a hotel's own website in eleven. For a European city hotel with a flat rate card, a price without a date is a rounding error. For a property running green season, shoulder and peak, with a permit window layered on top, a price without a date is simply a wrong price, and it is being quoted to a traveller in the planning phase where the decision gets made. ATPCO's own testing found AI agents answering flight-offer questions accurately about six per cent of the time, while a panel of Burgess, Niklas Andréen of Sabre and Domin put agentic booking at fifteen per cent to fifty per cent of airline tickets within two years. The inaccuracy and the adoption curve are arriving together.

The practical implication is narrower than the rhetoric around AI usually suggests. An East African operator cannot manufacture a 270 million member loyalty programme, cannot become merchant of record for a charter flight it does not operate, and cannot afford United's luxury of refusing a channel. It can make its rates machine-readable, date-specific and explicit about what they include, so that the eighteen-in-ninety-nine problem does not get solved against it by a model guessing. It can decide deliberately, rather than by default, which intermediary it is content to see nominated as accountable for its guest. And it can put its TRA licence number into its structured data now, on the reasonable bet that a protocol which currently has no field for regulatory standing will eventually need one, and that the suppliers who already publish it will be the ones a cautious agent can recommend.

June Chepkemei, the Kenya Tourism Board's chief executive, described her domestic campaign in September as a shift from raising awareness to "building direct pathways from interest to booking". The phrase was about Kenyans travelling in Kenya. It happens to be the exact problem the global industry spent three days in Florida failing to solve. Interest and booking have come apart, and the party that closes the gap will be the party the traveller believes will answer the phone.
