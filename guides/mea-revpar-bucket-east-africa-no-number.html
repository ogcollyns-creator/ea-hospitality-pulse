Hilton reported its Africa region down 5.6 per cent. It was not measuring Africa.

By Onyango George (OG)

Three of the four listed hotel majors reported the same overlapping geography this month. Hilton's Middle East and Africa line fell 5.6 per cent and is guided to a low double-digit decline for the year, IHG's wider EMEAA bucket rose 5.0 per cent, and Accor published its numbers twice, once with the Middle East in and once with it out. None of them published a figure for Africa. East Africa is where those same companies are building hardest, and it is the half of a hyphen whose number is set somewhere else.

Hilton published its third-quarter results on 27 October and raised its outlook for the year. Revenue per available room across the system was up 5.8 per cent on a currency-neutral basis. The United States was up 7.0 per cent, the Americas outside the United States up 6.3 per cent, Europe up 6.5 per cent. Asia Pacific was almost flat, down 0.5 per cent. One line in the table pointed the other way: Middle East and Africa, down 5.6 per cent.

On the call that followed, Kevin Jacobs, Hilton's chief financial officer, explained that line in a sentence that did not contain the word Africa. RevPAR in the region, he said, "declined 5.6% year-over-year as conflict related pressures in the region intensified during the quarter". He went further on the outlook: "We now expect full year RevPAR in the region to decline by a low double digit percentage." Christopher Nassetta, the chief executive, was blunter about the cause when asked why earnings had not flowed through as expected, saying the Middle East was "a little more beaten up" than the company had allowed for, and then offering the consolation that comes with a cyclical read: "I think The Middle East will be a heck of a lot better next year than this year."

For an owner in Nairobi, Arusha or Kigali, none of that is a curiosity about somebody else's war. It is, for this quarter, the only public performance number about their region that institutional capital is going to read.

Compare it with what the same geography looked like five days earlier. IHG issued its third-quarter trading update on 22 October: global RevPAR up 5.3 per cent in the quarter, up 4.6 per cent for the year to date, the Americas up 6.8 per cent, Greater China down 3.3 per cent, and the region that contains both Dubai and Dar es Salaam, which IHG calls EMEAA, up 5.0 per cent. Elie Maalouf, the chief executive, described what sat inside it as "a broadly flat performance in the Middle East", a drag the rest of the bucket comfortably absorbed.

Accor reported on the same day and did something more revealing. Group RevPAR was up 4.9 per cent, its Middle East, Africa and Asia-Pacific region up 3.6 per cent, and then every headline figure appeared a second time with the Middle East stripped out: the region up 4.0 per cent excluding the Middle East, the Luxury and Lifestyle division up 8.3 per cent but up 11.3 per cent excluding it, the Premium, Midscale and Economy division up 3.2 against 3.3. The release names the United Arab Emirates as the one market the conflict hit, still running about 20 per cent below the third quarter of 2025, and it notes that the Middle East and Africa sub-region accounts for a fifth of the region's room revenue. Sébastien Bazin, Accor's chairman and chief executive, said simply that "the Group delivered strong growth in the third quarter", which the ex-Middle East numbers support rather better than the headline ones.

Set the three side by side and the arithmetic stops being about hotels. In one calendar quarter, across heavily overlapping territory, the listed majors reported minus 5.6 per cent, plus 5.0 per cent and plus 3.6 per cent. That spread is not a measurement of how African hotels traded between July and September. It is a measurement of where each company drew a line on a map, how much Gulf exposure sits inside it, and whether anybody at head office thought the composite was honest enough to publish on its own. Accor's answer to that last question was no, which is why it published the number twice.

Marriott completes the set tomorrow morning, releasing third-quarter results at 7am Eastern on 2 November with a call at eight, hosted by Anthony Capuano and the chief financial officer, Jennifer Mason. Marriott is the largest hotel company in Africa's development pipeline. What it will not do, on the form of every quarter it has reported, is publish a RevPAR figure for Africa by itself.

Which is the part that should irritate East African owners, because these are the same companies currently placing their biggest African bets on their doorstep. W Hospitality Group's annual survey of chain development pipelines, published on 10 March, put the continental pipeline at a record 123,846 rooms across 675 hotels, up 18.6 per cent year on year and up 12.2 per cent on a same-store basis. Egypt alone holds 45,984 of those rooms, more than a third of the continent. But the headline number conceals the more useful one, which is how much of each country's pipeline has actually broken ground. Kenya has 6,190 rooms across 35 hotels, of which 79.5 per cent are under construction. Ethiopia has 5,964 rooms with 79.9 per cent under construction, Tanzania 4,159 rooms with 77.5 per cent. Trevor Ward, the firm's managing director, put it plainly: "What stands out this year is the strength of East Africa in terms of projects moving forward." Marriott leads the continental pipeline with 31,782 rooms, Hilton and Accor behind it, and the big five chains between them account for roughly 80 per cent of the pipeline.

So the position is this. The brands are converting East African signatures into concrete faster than they are anywhere else on the continent. They are also reporting the results of that region, when the hotels open, inside a composite whose quarterly direction is currently set by conflict several thousand kilometres from any of it. Hilton's own system-level numbers were strong: net unit growth of 6.0 per cent, a record pipeline of 543,100 rooms with nearly half under construction, new construction starts up more than 40 per cent year on year, guidance for the full year lifted from 3 to 3.5 per cent up to 4 to 4.5 per cent. Nassetta noted that conversions made up 37 per cent of the quarter's openings. None of that momentum gets attached, in any public document, to the countries doing the building.

The honest difficulty is that East Africa cannot answer with a like-for-like number, because it does not have one. Kenya's own data for the 2025/26 government financial year, announced by the Ministry of Tourism and Wildlife in August, records 2.76 million international arrivals, up about 9.5 per cent, and earnings of KSh564 billion, up about 18.5 per cent against a target of KSh529 billion, with average spend per international traveller rising from KSh189,000 to KSh204,298. Those are good numbers and they moved in the opposite direction to Hilton's regional line. They are also arrivals and receipts, not revenue per available room, measured over a financial year rather than a calendar quarter, which makes the comparison suggestive rather than decisive.

It is worth being equally honest about the softer part of the same release, which has been widely misreported. Domestic bed-nights came in at 5.15 million, up 2.6 per cent, which was 554,000 short of a 5.70 million target. The 5.7 million figure now circulating as an achievement was the target, not the outturn. As for an actual Kenyan RevPAR series, the nearest public thing is a sub-regional performance note on CoStar's site whose Kenyan figure is a RevPAR decline of 18.0 per cent, driven by weakness in Nairobi, for the month of March 2025. That is the most recent country-level rate-and-occupancy number a Kenyan hotelier can point a lender to without a subscription. It is nineteen months old.

That absence has a price, and it is not abstract. Regional composites are what gets quoted in franchise and management negotiations, what sets the mood of an incentive-fee discussion, what a valuer reaches for when there is no local series, what a credit committee reads when it has never financed a lodge, and what the trade press recycles for the following six months. An East African owner whose property traded well through the third quarter now carries a public regional headline of minus 5.6 per cent and a full-year guide to a low double-digit decline, neither of which they earned. The reciprocal risk arrives next year. If Nassetta is right that the Middle East will be "a heck of a lot better", the 2027 rebound in that composite will flatter East African assets that took no part in the fall and will take no part in the recovery, and a number nobody earned on the way down is a poor basis for a valuation on the way up.

Mohammed Hersi, a director of Pollman's Tours and Safaris and one of the more consistently unsentimental voices in Kenyan tourism, made a related point last month about the gap between headline growth and what operators actually experience. "It is not always about growth," he said. "It is also about stability." In a September column he framed the competitive question in terms that bear directly on this: "Kenya is not competing against itself. We are competing against Tanzania, against South Africa." That is true, and it is precisely the comparison the public data will not support. Sam Ikwaye, the Kenya Association of Hotelkeepers and Caterers coast executive officer, said of the sector's persistent constraints that "that is what is still blocking the growth that we should be seeing".

The remedy is unglamorous and sits with owners rather than ministries. Benchmarking subscriptions that produce a defensible competitive set cost a fraction of one month's brand fee, and a property that can show its own market data has something to put next to a regional composite in a fee negotiation. Incentive-fee thresholds and performance tests should be written against a named local competitive set rather than a brand's regional index, because a region whose number is set by a Gulf conflict is a hopeless benchmark in both directions. Association-level reporting, aggregated and anonymised, is the cheapest route to a credible national series and the only one that does not wait on legislation.

Hilton, IHG and Accor will keep reporting a region that does not function as a market, because segment reporting follows corporate structure and investor habit, not geography. That is their business. The question for East Africa is whether it keeps consenting to be the half of a hyphen, its best trading quarter in years disclosed to the world's capital as a minus sign belonging to somebody else's war.
