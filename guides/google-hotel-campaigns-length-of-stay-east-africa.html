Kenya is targeting a fourteen-night stay. Google's new hotel bid cannot tell one night from fourteen.

By Onyango George (OG)

A grey box at the top of Google's documentation says hotel campaigns migrate to Search campaigns for Travel from the fourth quarter of 2026. Three bid types do not come with them, including the one that set a bid as a percentage of the room price and multiplied it by the length of stay. East Africa sells the high-rate, long-duration stay that bid was built to price, and almost nobody at the lodges holds the account where the change lands.

A hotel advertiser who opened Google's own documentation on hotel campaigns this month found a grey box sitting above the heading, in the style Google reserves for things it would rather you read. "Important: Starting Q4 2026, Hotel campaigns will migrate to new Search campaigns for Travel," it says. Then, without elaboration: "Check your account notifications and email for precise dates." There is no press release attached, no date more precise than a quarter, and no explanation of what the word migrate is carrying. For a camp in the Mara or a beach hotel on Zanzibar's north coast, what it is carrying is the removal of the one bidding mechanism in paid search that understood what East African inventory is actually worth.

Hotel campaigns were built around a hotel's own prices. To run one, Google required three things, in its own wording: a "Hotel list (the hotels you want to advertise)", "Prices (an up-to-date set of prices for different itineraries)", and "Landing pages (pages on your website that users arrive at when clicking on your ads)". All three were managed in a Hotel Center account linked to Google Ads. Because the system already knew the rate, it could let an advertiser bid against it. One of the bid types available was CPC%, described by Google as follows: "Bid a percentage of the room price per night when a traveler clicks on your ad." Bids could then be adjusted, again in Google's words, "by factors such as device type, traveler location, length of stay".

That is the mechanism going away. Google's hotel campaigns page states plainly that in the replacement format, "Commission (per conversion), Max CPC 'percent' (%CPC), and Enhanced CPC are not available." It adds that in Search campaigns for Travel, "CPC is no longer multiplied by the length of stay." What remains is manual cost per click at a fixed amount, or the Smart Bidding suite: maximise conversions or conversion value, with an optional target return on ad spend or target cost per acquisition.

Simone Puorto, head of emerging trends and strategic innovation at Hospitality Net, and Alessio Re, co-founder of the consultancy Elegia, published an explainer on 23 November that put the arithmetic in a single line. "A €1 bid remains €1," they wrote, "whether the traveller searches for one night or seven."

Read that sentence from Nairobi rather than from Milan and it changes character. A European city hotel sells a one or two night stay at a moderate rate, and a flat click bid is a reasonable approximation of what a click is worth. East Africa sells the inverse, and says so officially. Kenya's draft National Tourism Strategy for 2025 to 2030, published by the Ministry of Tourism and Wildlife in June 2025, lists among its expected outcomes for 2030 an "Average length of stay 12-14 nights." The safari circuit is sold in multiples of nights, with minimum stays written into the rate sheet, at nightly rates that in the high-end camps run into several hundred dollars and beyond. A percentage bid on a high nightly rate, multiplied by a stay of a fortnight, converted the value of a click into a bid automatically, without anyone at the property opening a spreadsheet. The mechanism that was most generous to East African inventory is the one being withdrawn, and it is being withdrawn because of how it behaves in markets that look nothing like this one.

The migration mechanics matter more than the headline, and here the record is thinner than it should be. Antonio Fernandez, writing for the agency Relevant Audience on 18 November, reported that under automatic migration a CPC% campaign is converted to manual CPC "set at the entity's average CPC over the past 30 days", and that an advertiser who does nothing will have active hotel campaigns ended and replacements created automatically in mid first half of 2027. That second date sits awkwardly against Google's own "Starting Q4 2026", and neither of Google's pages carries a visible last-updated stamp to reconcile them. The agency account should be treated as reported rather than confirmed. If it is right, though, the consequence is worth stating slowly: the fixed bid a property carries into 2027 will be a photograph of whichever thirty days its account happened to be migrated in, and Google has said those dates arrive individually, by email. Late November into December is not the thirty-day window a Kenyan or Tanzanian operator would choose to be photographed in. It is the month in which rates soften, the short rains close roads, and Uganda prices its gorilla permits as low season. A bid struck on that basis then runs into the January to March peak.

The harder cutoff has already passed, and a good many operators will not have noticed it. Google's Hotel Center documentation on third-party rates says the feature "will be unavailable after September 30, 2026", and that hotel ads campaigns affected by the change "will stop serving ads across all Hotel Ads inventory (including Booking Module and Travel Promotion Ads)". The remedy is stated in the imperative: "To continue showing hotel ads, you must set up a direct price feed." That means working with an integration partner or opening a Hotel Center account, linking the feed, and recreating the campaigns with similar bidding and budgets. Until then, a Performance Max campaign keeps running on other surfaces, Search, Display and YouTube, using what Google calls "Google sourced third-party rates", over which the advertiser has no control: the rate shown cannot be changed and the booking link cannot be modified.

Go back to Google's requirement and read it as an East African supply question. "Prices (an up-to-date set of prices for different itineraries)." A large share of the region's most valuable beds cannot produce that sentence. The nine-night Serengeti and Zanzibar itinerary is priced by a consultant, by hand, on request, against availability held by telephone and confirmed by email. There is no live, machine-readable set of prices for different itineraries to hand over, because the commercial model never needed one. Google has now made exactly that artefact the condition of appearing on its hotel surfaces at all. It also reserves the right to be unimpressed by what it receives: the documentation says Google "automatically filters out any feeds with a low price accuracy score", and offers "no guarantee of full coverage".

The third problem is that in East Africa the person who will have to act on any of this is usually not the person who owns the beds. Puorto and Re make the point for independents generally: many do not manage hotel ads themselves, with campaigns run by booking engine providers, channel managers, metasearch specialists and agencies working to standardised bidding models. Providers who built their products on CPC%, they write, "will also need to revisit the commercial assumptions behind their products." The regional layer this describes is real and concentrated. HotelOnline, founded in Nairobi in 2014 by Endre Opdal and Håvar Bauck, sells managed distribution to online travel agencies, global distribution systems and metasearch engines for hotels across sub-Saharan Africa, and it is one of several such intermediaries. If the migration lands on the intermediary's commercial model, the owner's first sight of it will be a line in an invoice or a quiet fall in direct bookings three months later, with no document to point at.

What makes this regressive rather than merely disruptive is the threshold arithmetic. Puorto and Re note that target return on ad spend on Search wants "a minimum of 15 conversions with value over 30 days", and that data-driven attribution wants "200 conversions and 2,000 ad interactions over the same period". Their verdict is blunt: many independent hotels "may struggle to reach" those volumes. A twelve-tent camp does not transact two hundred direct bookings in a month, and will not, whatever it spends. So the smart bidding that is supposed to replace the manual lever is out of reach for precisely the properties losing the manual lever, which leaves them on flat manual CPC: the one setting that ignores both the room rate and the length of the stay. The chains, which clear the thresholds comfortably, keep an optimising system. The lodge keeps a number.

There is a measurement loss folded into the same change, and it is the part an operator can still do something about this week. Travel promotion ads will count impressions the way standard Search and Shopping do, which means a card can register an impression when its carousel is displayed even if the traveller never scrolls to it. Puorto and Re warn that "reported impressions could increase while CTR and impression-share metrics deteriorate", and that "top impression share and viewed impressions will no longer be available for Travel Promotion Ads". Their recommendation is to bank "at least 12-18 months of historical reporting" before switching, segmented by format, hotel group, length of stay and days to check-in. For an East African property that is not a technical nicety. Without that export, a soft first quarter in 2027 cannot be told apart from a market that moved, a levy that landed, an advisory that was reissued, or a bid that quietly stopped reflecting a fortnight's stay.

Google's own case for the destination format should be put on the record alongside all this, and weighed for what it is. Its documentation claims advertisers see "an average increase of 18% total incremental conversions at a similar cost per action". That is a vendor claim on a vendor page with no study attached, and an average drawn across a global advertiser base in which the heavy spenders are the chains and the online travel agencies. It is not evidence about a twenty-room property in Arusha, and should not be read as any.

None of this is an announcement about East Africa. That is the recurring shape of these stories: a bidding change designed around short-stay urban inventory in high-volume markets propagates to a region whose entire commercial proposition is the long, expensive, hand-assembled stay. The question for an operator this week is narrow and answerable. Who holds the Hotel Center account that carries your property, on what basis do they bid, has anyone exported the last eighteen months of reporting, and if your campaign is on CPC% today, what flat number will replace it and which thirty days was it taken from. Nobody is going to write to the owner about this. Google has said the emails go to the account.
