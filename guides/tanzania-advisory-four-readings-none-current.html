Four governments have read Tanzania's year. Not one of them has read it lately.

By Onyango George (OG)

Tanzania went to London this week to sell eight million tourists, then a court in Dar es Salaam jailed the opposition leader. The commercial problem is not the verdict: it is that the four advisory pages which price that risk have all stopped being updated.

The Tanzanian stand at ExCeL closed on Thursday. Dr Ashatu Kijaji, the natural resources and tourism minister, had spent the week of World Travel Market in London with more than thirty Tanzanian companies around her, telling them that an exhibition is only worth the commercial relationships it produces, and repeating the number the government has attached to the next five years: eight million tourists. The stand came down, the delegation flew home, and on Friday a three-judge panel of the High Court in Dar es Salaam dismissed the treason charge against Tundu Lissu, convicted him of incitement, and sentenced him to four years.

"We are satisfied that through his statements, the accused intended to disrupt elections and instigate violence," the court held. Lissu, who leads Chadema and was arrested before the October 2025 presidential election, called the trial political persecution. "Excuses were created to imprison me," he said. Tito Magoti, a Tanzanian human rights lawyer based in Oxford, read the dismissal of the treason count as "a major blow" for the state, on the argument that the object of the exercise had been imprisonment rather than a treason conviction. The verdict drew calls for Lissu's release from British and European Union parliamentarians.

For a camp on the Serengeti's western corridor or a thirty-key beach hotel in Paje, the question raised by that Friday is not whether the judgment was sound. It is narrower, and more awkward. Tanzania's 2027 season is being contracted now, in sales halls and on agent calls, and the risk attached to that season is not priced by the court. It is priced by four pages of government prose, one each from Washington, Canberra, Ottawa and London, which sit behind every corporate travel policy, university risk committee, donor travel rule and cancellation clause that touches the destination. Read those four pages this weekend and the striking thing is not that they disagree about Tanzania. It is that not one of them is a current reading.

Start with the one that carries the most commercial weight. The United States has Tanzania at Level 3, Reconsider Travel, raised from Level 2 with an unrest indicator added. That advisory is dated 31 October 2025. It is still the live page. It warns that "Demonstrations often take place in response to political or economic issues" and that "Demonstrations can be unpredictable", notes that violent crime is common and that the terrorism risk is concentrated in the Mtwara Region, and assigns no separate level to any part of the country. It does not mention Zanzibar at all. Twelve months have passed since it was written. In that time the islands posted their highest ever July, and the file has not been reopened.

Australia's page is more arresting still, because it is written in the present tense. Canberra advises travellers to "Reconsider your need to travel overall due to civil unrest and violent protests". It states that "Authorities announced a curfew in Dar es Salaam and more curfews may be enforced at short notice", and that "Protests, demonstrations, movement restrictions and roadblocks are disrupting traffic and public transportation". Not disrupted. Are disrupting. An Australian who opened that page on Friday afternoon was told that roadblocks were interfering with public transport in Tanzania at that moment. The page carries no visible date of review.

Canada looked at its Tanzania file on 30 October 2026, at half past ten in the morning Eastern time, and the published note on what changed reads: removed information on demonstrations. Ottawa holds the country at exercise a high degree of caution, with avoid all travel inside ten kilometres of the Mozambique border in Mtwara and avoid non-essential travel across a wider strip. The safety section still carries the sentence "Protests following previous elections resulted in fatalities", and still warns that demonstrations "can occur anywhere across the country and sometimes on short notice". So Canada reviewed the politics a week before the verdict and took some of it out.

The United Kingdom reviewed the same file on the same day. London's only advice against travel is for areas within twenty kilometres of the border with Mozambique's Cabo Delgado province, on grounds of attacks by groups linked with Islamic extremism. There is no political situation section. There is no demonstrations language. There is no mention of Zanzibar. The change the Foreign Office published on 30 October 2026 was additional information about using security measures to reduce the risk of burglary in hotels and other accommodation. The advice was marked current as at 5 November 2026, the day Tanzania's stand in London came down.

Two governments edited their Tanzania pages on the same Friday in late October, and between them they produced one note about hotel burglary and one deletion of the word demonstrations. A third has not touched the file since the week of the unrest itself. The fourth still describes roadblocks as an ongoing condition. Four jurisdictions, one country, four irreconcilable answers, and no evidence that any of them reflects a fresh assessment of the destination as it actually stood in November 2026.

This matters for a specific and unglamorous reason. The advisory is not really read by holidaymakers, who take their cue from headlines, from their agent, and from whoever went last year. It is read by the people who write policies. A level is a machine-readable input: it goes into a corporate travel approval workflow, a university field-research sign-off, an insurer's underwriting note, a tour operator's duty-of-care file, the risk annexe of a conference bid. Nobody publishes a count of how many institutions treat a Level 3 as an automatic trigger, and this piece does not claim one. What is observable is the shape of the problem: the institutions that need a single number get one, and in Tanzania's case the number they get was set in the first week of November 2025 and has not been examined since.

The destination's own accounts tell a quieter story than any of the four pages. The Bank of Tanzania's Monthly Economic Review for September 2026 puts travel receipts for the year ending August 2026 at 4,389.7 million dollars, against 4,314.7 million for the year ending August 2025 and 3,767.7 million for the year ending August 2024. The central bank describes this as continued resilience in tourism, and on the level it is right. On the trend it is something else. Those three figures describe growth of roughly 14.5 per cent in the year that ended in August 2025 and roughly 1.7 per cent in the year that ended in August 2026. The percentages are arithmetic on the bank's own dollar amounts rather than figures it publishes, but the direction is not in doubt. Arrivals in the year to February 2026 were 2,255,006, up 4.2 per cent on the bank's own count.

That is the pattern to understand. The guests did not leave. The growth did. A destination can hold its volume and lose its momentum, and momentum is what pays for the new wing, the second camp, the vehicle replacement cycle and the refinancing. Zanzibar, meanwhile, recorded 107,801 visitors in July 2026, its highest ever figure for that month. The islands are not in a crisis, which is precisely why the gap between what Zanzibar's own statistics say and what an Australian traveller is told has become a commercial problem in its own right.

Above the advisories sits a slower layer that does not get revised at all, only compounded. On 1 November, five days before the verdict, the Mo Ibrahim Foundation published its 2026 governance index, which found 23 African countries worse governed over the decade to 2025, 30 better and one unchanged, with gains in economic opportunity and human development set against falls in security, human rights and participation. Asked about the recent elections in Tanzania and Uganda, Mo Ibrahim was blunt. "It's just disgraceful," he said. "It is a strange era," he added of the wider pattern. "We can see this phenomenon of the rise of strongmen everywhere." The EastAfrican, reporting the index on Friday, noted Kenya and Rwanda among the continent's top twenty on overall governance. Tanzania was not in that sentence. Index scores do not cancel bookings next week. They shape which destinations appear on a corporate shortlist in three years.

So what should an operator selling 2027 actually do with this. Four things, none of them about politics.

Find out which page your source markets read, and learn its edit date. An operator who can tell a German or British agent that the Foreign Office reviewed Tanzania on 30 October 2026 and said nothing whatever about the political situation is offering a verifiable fact rather than a reassurance, and the fact is stronger. Second, pre-write the Level 3 answer, because every American corporate, academic and donor enquiry will arrive carrying it. A single page that gives the advisory's date, states what it does and does not cover, sets the other three readings beside it and cites Zanzibar's July arrivals will clear more approvals than an argument about fairness. Third, read your own insurance and cancellation wording for the phrase government advisory against travel, and establish which government. Tanzania currently supplies four different answers to that question, and a clause that does not name its jurisdiction is a dispute waiting for a claim. Fourth, do not litigate the verdict with the trade. The weakest point in the American page is not its judgment of Tanzania. It is that the judgment is twelve months old.

The thing to watch over the coming weeks is not a downgrade. A downgrade would at least be a reading, and a reading can be argued with. The risk going into the Zanzibar and northern-circuit high season is duller than that: that four governments stopped looking at roughly the same time, that the page a procurement officer in Chicago opens in the first week of November 2026 was written in the first week of November 2025, and that nothing in Friday's judgment will prompt anyone to open the file again. Tanzania spent last week in London selling eight million tourists. It is simultaneously described, in one of the four jurisdictions that price its risk, as a country where roadblocks are disrupting public transport. Both statements are on the record. Only one of them is being kept up to date.
