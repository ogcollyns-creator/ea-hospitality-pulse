The world's largest seller of hotel rooms guided its next quarter down to 1 per cent. It did not blame the room.

By Onyango George (OG)

Booking Holdings reported 7 per cent room-night growth on 3 November and told the market to expect 1 to 3 per cent in the fourth quarter, naming elevated airfares and reduced route capacity on long-haul routes as the cause. East Africa sells almost nothing but long-haul. The same month's aviation data shows African carriers adding international capacity faster than they fill it, at the lowest load factor of any region in the world.

At the ExCeL centre in London on 3 November, with more than forty Kenyan trade partners working the stands around her, the chief executive of the Kenya Tourism Board was selling a number. June Chepkemei's pitch at World Travel Market was that Kenya should take 199,645 British visitors this year against 170,936 in 2025, a rise of 16.8 per cent, and that it would get there by widening the offer beyond the thing Britain already associates with the country. "Our pitch is that Kenya offers much more than the iconic safari experience," she said.

Later the same day, several thousand kilometres west, the company that sells more hotel rooms than any other on earth told its investors something that bears directly on whether a number like that is achievable. Booking Holdings reported its third quarter: room nights up 7 per cent year on year, an acceleration from 5 per cent in the second quarter, gross bookings of $54.5bn up about 11 per cent in constant currency, average daily rates up around 3 per cent. By the standards of a sector that has spent 2026 apologising for its comparatives, it was a good quarter. Then the chief financial officer, Ewout Steenbergen, gave the guidance. "We currently expect fourth quarter room nights to increase between 1% and 3%," he said.

That is a deceleration from roughly 7 per cent to roughly 2 per cent at the midpoint, inside one quarter, at the business that is the single largest intermediary for East African accommodation. What makes it worth 1,500 words in Nairobi, Arusha and Kigali rather than in Norwalk, Connecticut, is the reason Steenbergen gave for it. Part of the answer was arithmetic: the guidance reflects, in his words, "the toughest prior year comparison of 2026". But he did not stop there. "We saw some moderation in global demand trends toward the end of the third quarter, driven by international travel," he said, "with elevated airfares and reduced capacity on certain routes weighing on long-haul travel demand." The outlook, he added, "assumes that both the direct and indirect impacts of the Middle East conflict on travel persists".

Read that sentence again as an East African operator. The world's largest room seller has identified where its demand is softening, and it is not in the room. It is in the journey. The softness is in international, long-haul traffic, and the mechanism is the price and availability of the seat.

East Africa's high-spend business is nothing but international long-haul traffic. Europe accounted for 691,194 Kenyan arrivals in 2025 and 28.67 per cent of the total, the second-largest source region after Africa itself, and Britain alone supplied 170,936 of them. Every one of those guests buys a flight before they buy a bed, most of them through a hub in the Gulf or in western Europe, and the room is the second line on an invoice whose first line the operator does not control.

The composition of Booking's quarter sharpens the point. What held the company's 7 per cent up was not the kind of travel East Africa sells. Steenbergen's regional breakdown ran: "Europe grew high single digits with domestic room nights up high single digits", "Asia grew high single digits with domestic room nights up low double digits", and "the U.S. grew mid-single digits, driven by domestic room night growth". The resilient demand in the world's largest accommodation book this quarter was people sleeping in their own countries. The segment that moderated towards the end of the period was the segment that crosses oceans. An East African lodge is positioned almost entirely in the second category and almost not at all in the first.

So far this reads as a straightforward warning, and it would be dishonest to leave it there, because the aviation data published five days earlier says something that cuts the other way. The International Air Transport Association's September figures, released on 30 October, show total global passenger demand down 0.5 per cent year on year, international demand up only 0.4 per cent, and the whole of that weakness concentrated in two places. Middle Eastern carriers saw international demand fall 7.5 per cent. Strip the Middle East out and global international demand rose 1.7 per cent rather than 0.4. Marie Owens Thomsen, IATA's senior vice president for sustainability and chief economist, put the diagnosis plainly: "Air traffic demand fell 0.5% in September, reflecting weaknesses in the Middle East and North America."

African airlines were not in that weakness. Their international demand rose 8.0 per cent in September, up from 6.7 per cent in August, the second-strongest regional performance in the world behind Latin America's 8.2 per cent, and the ninth consecutive month of growth on that measure. Whatever is happening to long-haul travel globally, it is not currently showing up as a collapse in people flying to and from Africa.

What it is showing up as is something more awkward, and it sits in the next column of the same table. African international capacity grew 8.8 per cent in September against that 8.0 per cent demand growth. The regional load factor was 74.8 per cent, down 0.6 points on a year earlier and down from 78.4 per cent in August. That is the lowest load factor of any region in the world. The global international figure was 83.6 per cent. European carriers flew at 86.1 per cent. Middle Eastern carriers, in the middle of a 7.5 per cent demand collapse, still filled 78.3 per cent of their seats, three and a half points better than Africa managed while growing.

Put the two disclosures side by side and the position becomes legible. East Africa does not have a seat shortage. It has the emptiest aircraft on the planet, and it has them because capacity has been added faster than demand for most of this year. What it has instead is a buyer who, according to the largest distributor of accommodation in the world, has begun to hesitate at the total price of a long journey. Those two facts together are worse than either alone, because they mean the hesitation is happening over a cost East African operators cannot reduce, on routes where the empty seats are already evidence that nobody in the chain has the pricing power to fix it by discounting.

The timing is the part that should concentrate minds. Booking's fourth-quarter guidance is a forecast about stays, and East Africa's fourth quarter is largely already sold: the December to February season sits on the books. The hesitation Steenbergen described is not about those stays. It is about what people are doing in the shopping window right now, which is the same window in which East Africa's trade spent this week in London selling July and August 2027. A long-haul safari is bought months ahead of the night it is slept in. The quarter in which a distributor reports moderating international demand is therefore not the quarter the damage appears in East African occupancy. It is the quarter the damage is contracted.

There are two honest limits on how far this can be pushed. The first is mix. Booking's room nights are overwhelmingly European and Asian city and domestic stays, with alternative accommodation now around 37 per cent of the total and listings up 9 per cent to 9.4 million. East African safari and beach product is a rounding error inside that book, and the company's guidance is not a forecast for Kenya, Tanzania or Rwanda. It is a read on the behaviour of the world's largest pool of accommodation shoppers, which is a different and more useful thing than a destination forecast, but it is not a substitute for one. The second is the comparative. Steenbergen was explicit that the fourth quarter is the company's hardest comparison of the year, so some unknown share of the drop from 7 per cent to 1 or 3 per cent is arithmetic rather than appetite. Expedia Group reports its own third quarter today, and a second distributor describing the same long-haul moderation would turn a signal into a pattern. One that does not would leave this as a single company's conservatism.

What should not be in dispute is that East African operators have no forward demand series of their own. Kenya's published numbers are good and they are backward-looking: 2.76 million international arrivals and KSh564bn in earnings for the 2025/26 financial year, reported in August, measured over a period that ended in June. Nothing in the region publishes a quarterly, forward-looking, publicly filed view of what accommodation buyers are about to do. Two listed distributors and one airline trade body do, every quarter, for free, and the region reads none of them as an operating input.

Mohammed Hersi, a director of Pollman's Tours and Safaris, made the relevant point about the gap between a growth headline and a trading reality last month. "It is not always about growth," he said. "It is also about stability." Sam Ikwaye of the Kenya Association of Hotelkeepers and Caterers put the coast's version of it more bluntly, saying of the sector's persistent constraints that "that is what is still blocking the growth that we should be seeing".

The practical response is unglamorous and sits with operators rather than ministries. If the buyer is hesitating over the total cost of the trip rather than the nightly rate, then the nightly rate is the wrong lever and discounting it transfers margin to an airline without changing the decision. The lever is the total. That means quoting all in, with park fees, levies and the mandatory insurance gates included rather than disclosed late, so the number the guest hesitates over is one the operator has actually optimised. It means treating a 74.8 per cent regional load factor as negotiating leverage rather than as somebody else's statistic, because block space and joint fare-plus-lodge pricing are cheaper to buy from a carrier with empty seats than from one without. It means taking seriously that the resilient demand in the global data is short-haul and domestic, which is the one part of the East African book that matches the world's pattern rather than fighting it. And it means putting Booking's and Expedia's quarterly guidance ranges in the same file as the arrivals bulletins, because they arrive months earlier and they are the only forward numbers available.

Chepkemei's 16.8 per cent is a reasonable ambition and the British market has the money to deliver it. The question the week's two disclosures pose is not whether Britain wants to come to Kenya. It is whether the journey still prices as if it does, and whether anyone selling the room has priced the journey into the conversation at all.
