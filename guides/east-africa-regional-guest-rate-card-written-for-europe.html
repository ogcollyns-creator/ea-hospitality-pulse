Africa is now 47 per cent of Kenya's arrivals. The rate card is still written for Europe.

By Onyango George (OG)

Seventy Ugandan hoteliers spent six days on the Kenyan coast this week and three trade associations signed memoranda in Mombasa, but the region's largest source market is the one East Africa's commercial architecture, priced in dollars and sold through overseas intermediaries, is least equipped to serve.

The delegation had already seen the product by the time the speeches began. More than seventy Ugandan tour operators, travel agents, hoteliers and Uganda Tourism Board officials had spent six days working their way along the Kenyan coast, playing a golf tournament at Vipingo Ridge on the Saturday, before assembling on Monday at the Sarova Whitesands Beach Resort in Mombasa for the fifth Uganda-Kenya Coast Tourism and Innovation Summit. Herbert Kiguli, Uganda's Consul General in Mombasa, framed the week in the language the region now uses by default. "Our approach is to embrace complementarity rather than competition," he said.

Three memoranda were signed on the opening day. Uganda's and Kenya's tour operator associations, AUTO and KATO, agreed on regional cooperation. Their travel agent counterparts, TUGATA and KATA, signed an implementation agreement for the coastal collaboration. The Private Sector Foundation Uganda signed with the Mombasa chapter of the Kenya National Chamber of Commerce and Industry. All three were private sector documents. A joint government declaration for the East African Community Secretariat was expected, and a communiqué with institutional commitments is due within a fortnight.

Behind the ceremony sits a number that should unsettle anyone selling beds on the Kenyan coast. In 2025, according to Uganda Tourism Board figures cited at the summit, 519,041 Kenyans visited Uganda, up from 466,902 the year before. Over the same period, Kenya Tourism Board data puts Ugandan arrivals in Kenya at 234,556, up from 201,623. Uganda is Kenya's second-largest source market, and the flow is still running better than two to one in Uganda's favour. Kenya owns the beach, the long-haul airlift and the brand, and it is a net exporter of travellers to the neighbour that has neither coast nor hub.

That asymmetry is not the anomaly. It is the shape of the market, arriving faster than the industry's assumptions about it. Kenya's own Tourism Sector Performance Report for 2025, released in April, put international arrivals at 2.7 million, up 9 per cent, with Africa accounting for 47 per cent of them against 25 per cent from Europe and 14 per cent from the Americas. A year earlier, the Tourism Research Institute's 2024 report recorded 2,394,376 arrivals of which 975,883, or 40.8 per cent, came from Africa. Six percentage points of structural shift in twelve months is not noise. In the same 2024 table, Uganda contributed 225,559 arrivals and Tanzania 203,290. Together the two neighbours out-delivered the United States, Kenya's single largest market at 306,501, by a comfortable margin, and nobody writes a marketing brief about them.

Uganda has already finished the journey Kenya is halfway through. Its Ministry of Tourism, Wildlife and Antiquities, presenting the Tourism Development Programme Performance Review for the 2025/26 financial year in Kampala this month, reported 1,642,215 international arrivals in 2025, a rise of 19.7 per cent, and receipts of US$1.62bn, up 21.3 per cent. Africa supplied 79.2 per cent of those arrivals, and the East African Community supplied 69.1 per cent of the African share. Asia accounted for 7.8 per cent, Europe 7.1 per cent, the Americas 4.2 per cent. India, at 57,051 arrivals, was the leading overseas market, ahead of the United States at 52,290. "Tourism continues to contribute significantly to foreign exchange earnings, employment, household incomes and investment," said Doreen S. Katusiime, the ministry's permanent secretary.

The reflex objection is that regional volume is cheap volume. Uganda's figures do not support it. Average length of stay rose to 8.8 nights, the highest in the review period. Average spend per international trip rose from US$933 to US$986, and average spend by leisure tourists from US$2,114 to US$2,144. A destination that is four-fifths African is also a destination where the typical visitor stays nearly nine nights and spends close to a thousand dollars. Whatever is dragging down the blended average, it is not the regional guest in aggregate.

For the mature version of this market, look south. Statistics South Africa released its September figures on Wednesday, and they describe a tourism economy that long ago stopped pretending Europe was the main event. Of roughly 835,000 tourists recorded in the month, 659,613, close to four in five, came from SADC countries: Mozambique with 182,751, Zimbabwe with 178,966, Lesotho with 127,152. Overseas tourists numbered 161,753, among them 27,825 Americans, 22,305 Britons and 17,627 Germans. An earlier Stats SA report this year put the SADC share at 78.2 per cent. The pattern has held for long enough that South African operators build around it rather than apologise for it.

One detail in that release is a warning to anyone reaching for a pan-African thesis. Kenya sent 3,874 tourists to South Africa in September and Uganda sent 1,759. Intra-African travel is not continental, it is a neighbourhood business, built on road borders, shared languages, family networks and a weekend's driving distance. East Africa's version of that market is the Busia and Malaba crossings, the Nairobi to Kampala coach, the Namanga road, the Dar to Zanzibar ferry. It is not reached by a stand at a trade fair in Berlin.

Which is precisely where the commercial problem begins, and why three association memoranda, however well meant, address the wrong constraint. The regional guest does not need to be told that Diani exists. The regional guest needs to be able to buy it. East Africa's hospitality architecture was engineered for the overseas 20 to 40 per cent: rates published in dollars, contracted months ahead through tour operators and online travel agencies, secured by a card-not-present deposit, bundled into multi-night itineraries, and signalled through a classification system whose stars were designed to reassure a buyer in Frankfurt. Almost every element of that machinery actively obstructs a Kampala family deciding on a Tuesday to drive to Mombasa on a Friday.

The state, oddly, got there first. Kenya Wildlife Service publishes its conservation fees in explicit tiers. At Amboseli, a non-resident adult pays US$90. A Kenya resident pays Ksh 2,025, and an East African citizen pays Ksh 1,500. At Nairobi National Park and Tsavo East the East African citizen rate is Ksh 1,000 against US$80 for a non-resident. The parks decided years ago that the regional visitor is a distinct customer with a distinct price in a distinct currency, and they enforce it at the gate. Many hotels have the equivalent tier buried in a rate sheet. Far fewer have it bookable: visible on the property's own site, payable by mobile money, confirmable without a card, available for two nights rather than five, and priced in the currency the guest actually earns.

Transport is the second blockage, and Kiguli named it. He pointed to high air fares as a major obstacle and proposed that the region be treated as a single aviation bloc. The ambition is right and the timing is poor: a regional guest who finds the Entebbe to Mombasa fare absurd simply drives, and a drive-in guest arrives late, unannounced, with luggage and children and no reservation reference. That is a yield management problem dressed as a connectivity problem, and it is solved at the front desk and in the channel manager long before it is solved at a ministerial summit.

The policy track is moving, slowly. Organisers in Mombasa plan a review of Article 104 of the EAC Treaty, the free movement provision, to identify what actually stops people crossing. Beatrice Askul Moe, Kenya's Cabinet Secretary for East African Community, ASALs and Regional Development, put the standard plainly: "Regional integration is measured by how easily our citizens, goods and services move across our borders." Rebecca Alitwala Kadaga, Uganda's First Deputy Prime Minister and EAC Affairs Minister, gave the keynote and asked for more than warm words. "Tourism cooperation must go beyond promoting destinations to empowering local communities," she said, calling for formal frameworks, business-friendly policy, better transport and improved visitor safety. "This summit must turn our good intentions into actions the rest of the Community can follow." Susan Nakawuki Nsambu, Uganda's Minister of State for Tourism, Wildlife and Antiquities, was blunter about the limits of the format. "The value of this partnership lies not simply in the meetings we hold," she said.

Two cautions are worth stating before anyone rewrites a budget. The cross-border figures come from two different national agencies counting two different things, Uganda Tourism Board counting Kenyan visitors and Kenya Tourism Board counting Ugandan arrivals, and neither separates leisure from trade, transit, family visits or cross-border work. The two-to-one imbalance is real in the published numbers and should not yet be read as a pure leisure deficit. And the regional guest, once courted properly, is rate-transparent in a way the long-haul guest is not. They will see the tier, compare it with the dollar rate, book the cheaper one, and talk about it. A property that opens a regional price without ring-fencing it by lead time, day of week and length of stay will discover it has not found a new market, it has repriced its old one.

Set against that, the structural case is now hard to argue with. Africa supplies 47 per cent of Kenya's arrivals and 79.2 per cent of Uganda's. The East African Community alone accounts for more than two-thirds of Uganda's African visitors. South Africa has run a four-to-one regional-to-overseas ratio for years and treats it as the business rather than the fallback. Seventy Ugandan buyers walked the Kenyan coast this month, and more than 450 stakeholders have joined these familiarisation trips since 2022, with Kenyan operators travelling to Uganda next week.

The operator question for Monday morning is therefore narrow and testable. Not whether the property markets to Kampala or Kigali or Arusha, which it probably already does badly and cheaply. The question is whether a family in Kampala can find a two-night rate, in shillings, on the property's own site, pay a deposit by mobile money without a card, and have it honoured on a Friday evening road arrival with no voucher. Where the answer is no, the fastest-growing source market in the region is being counted at the border and turned away at the till.
