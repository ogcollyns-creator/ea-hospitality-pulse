Ethiopia's north went dark in a night. East Africa's exposure is the map, not the airport.

By Onyango George (OG)

Tigrayan forces took three airports on 23 September and Washington now tells Americans to leave Amhara, Tigray and Afar; for East Africa's hotels the risk is not Addis Ababa's hub but the itinerary, the buyer's map and a neighbourhood that is getting louder.

The taxi driver was waiting for a fare at Mekelle's airport when the fighters arrived. They carried light weapons, he told the Associated Press, and ordered him to leave because no flights would land. He heard no gunfire. By the afternoon of 23 September, Ethiopian Airlines, the only carrier flying scheduled services into Tigray, had suspended its flights to Mekelle, Axum and Shire. "We sincerely apologize to our customers for any inconvenience caused by this suspension of flights," the airline said. "We will inform you once flight operations resume."

Nothing in that statement would trouble a lodge owner in the Mara or a hotelier in Stone Town. The Tigray routes are short domestic hops, flown with Dash 8 turboprops to Axum and Shire and Boeing 737s to Mekelle, according to AeroTime. They are not the aircraft that carry a German couple to Kilimanjaro. But the airports were a symptom, and the condition behind them reaches East Africa's order books by routes most operators have not yet mapped.

The sequence took less than a week. On Sunday 20 September the Tigray People's Liberation Front announced an alliance with six other armed groups, among them the Fano militia in Amhara, with the declared aim of overthrowing Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government. On the Wednesday the airports went. Getachew Reda, an adviser to the prime minister and himself a former TPLF leader, wrote on X that the front had "taken over federally controlled airports in Tigray leading to the suspension of flights to and from Tigray." He added: "The TPLF politico-military leadership has obviously taken irresponsibility to new heights." Tigray's regional government told a different story. Its forces, it said, were answering "intensive bombardments" by "aircraft, drones, and artillery", and had "entered into a defensive battle."

By Thursday Ethiopian had reportedly also stopped flying to Lalibela in Amhara, where Reuters had reported federal forces firing heavy weapons from a camp in the town. On Friday 25 September the US Embassy in Addis Ababa issued the sentence that matters most to anyone who sells travel. "U.S. citizens are advised not to travel to the Amhara Region (including Lalibela), the Tigray Region, or the Afar Region for any reason," it said, and those already there should "leave if it is safe to do so or shelter in place." Canada's foreign ministry said it was "disturbed by the seizure of the Mekelle airport and the telecom blackout." London had moved a day earlier. The Foreign Office's advice, updated on 24 September and read directly for this article, now warns against all travel to the parts of Afar within 50km of the Tigray boundary and 20km of the Eritrean border, and against all but essential travel to the rest of Afar. That sits on top of standing advice against all travel to Tigray and to Amhara.

Diplomats reached for the language of alarm. Mahmoud Ali Youssouf, chairperson of the African Union Commission, said the seizure and occupation of "the civilian airports of Mekelle, Axum, and Shire by Tigray forces" was inconsistent with the Pretoria Agreement of 2022 and risked "eroding the hard-won gains achieved in the pursuit of lasting peace and stability." He asked Olusegun Obasanjo, the former Nigerian president, to engage the parties urgently. The stakes are not abstract. The last war, from 2020 to 2022, killed about 600,000 people by African Union estimates, and about a million people in the north remain displaced.

The regional dimension is what should make East Africans sit up. Billene Seyoum, the prime minister's spokesperson, told Reuters that the new alliance had been working with financial backing from Eritrea, which has repeatedly denied such accusations. Alan Boswell, Horn of Africa director at the International Crisis Group, pointed to Eritrea's closeness to Sudan's army and to Ethiopia's own souring relations with it. "So this is all becoming one big regional mess," he told Al Jazeera, "and it could get very, very ugly if we don't see de-escalation." Ethiopia's president, Taye Atske-Selassie, used the United Nations General Assembly to send the opposite message. "Our message to forces of destruction and mayhem is loud and clear," he said, urging them to respect the right of the Horn's people "to live in peace and prosperity."

The instinctive East African question is about the hub. Addis Ababa is home to Ethiopian Airlines and one of the doors through which long-haul guests reach Entebbe, Kigali, Kilimanjaro and Zanzibar. It is also, for now, the question with the most reassuring answer. Nothing in this week's notices touches the capital or Bole International. The British map, which lists in painstaking detail the Ethiopian regions, zones and border strips to avoid, carves out the corridors around Addis Ababa, including the expressway to Adama and ten kilometres either side of the road to Bishoftu. The suspensions Ethiopian announced cover northern domestic points. When the airline does change its long-haul network, it tends to do so for commercial reasons that owners should be tracking anyway: this publication reported on 23 September that the Addis Ababa to Chicago service, routed via Rome, will drop to five flights a week from 18 January.

That is the misread. The exposure for East African hotels does not run through the runway at Bole. It runs through three other channels, and each of them is already live.

The first is the itinerary. Lalibela's rock-hewn churches and the stelae of Axum are UNESCO World Heritage sites, and both now sit behind the warnings. For British travellers that chapter was already closed on paper, since the Foreign Office had advised against all travel to Amhara before this week, and its own pages warn that travelling against its advice can invalidate insurance. What changed is that the aircraft stopped and Washington used the words "for any reason". Wherever an agent has paired Lalibela or Axum with a safari further south, the Ethiopian leg is now unsellable for the season, and the nights it held are looking for a home.

This is the part of the story nobody in the East African trade has yet said aloud. The obvious substitutes for a heritage chapter are on the Swahili coast. Stone Town in Zanzibar and Lamu Old Town are both World Heritage sites, both a short flight from the safari circuit. And by a coincidence of timing, on 25 September France redrew its map of Kenya. The Quai d'Orsay's security page, updated that day and read directly for this article, now places the islands of Lamu and Manda in its lowest band, "vigilance renforcée", with access by air only. On the map France had used since March, and which this publication carried until this week, the Lamu archipelago sat in orange. Paris did not move because of Ethiopia. But an agent re-routing a displaced week this autumn will find Lamu cleaner on the French map than at any point since at least March.

The second channel is the buyer's map, and here the news is less comfortable. Advisories bleed across borders in the mind of the person who signs off a trip. Kenya's frontier with Ethiopia already sits inside every major government's no-go zones: Britain advises against all travel within 10km of the Kenyan border on the Ethiopian side, and the US keeps the parts of Marsabit and Turkana within 50km of Ethiopia at Level 4, its highest. A duty-of-care officer at a European company or charity now sees "Ethiopia" and "fighting" in the same week that France keeps an active pre-electoral warning on Kenya, published on 11 September, ahead of the general election on 10 August 2027. The same officer may also have read the US Embassy in Nairobi's security alert of 25 September, which warned of a possible breach of personal data collected during Ebola screening at Kenya's ports of entry. "The U.S. Embassy is unable to confirm if an individual's data is part of the breach," it said. Among those potentially affected, according to the alert as reported by Kenyans.co.ke, is anyone who entered Kenya from Ethiopia since screening began in late May.

None of these items is large on its own. Together they describe a Horn of Africa that sounds louder than the booking data may justify, and Nairobi's conference and corporate trade lives on precisely those sign-offs. The advisories themselves did not move for Kenya this week. The British, American, Canadian and German advice on Kenya was unchanged when read directly on 26 September. That is a fact sales teams should be able to state in one sentence, with the dates, before a client asks.

The third channel is time. The peace that brought flights back to Tigray was slow to translate into air service: Ethiopian resumed Addis Ababa to Mekelle on 28 December 2022, weeks after the Pretoria deal, and daily flights to Axum returned only on 9 June 2024, after repairs to the airfield and terminal, according to AeroTime. If the pattern holds, a new war in the north is measured in seasons, not weeks. East African operators should plan on that basis rather than on a quick return to normal.

For owners, the conclusions are practical rather than dramatic. City hotels in Nairobi, Kampala and Kigali should brief their sales and reservations teams tonight with a short, dated statement: which Ethiopian regions are affected, that Addis Ababa and Bole sit outside every warning issued this week, and that the major advisories on their own country did not change. Beach properties in Lamu, Zanzibar and along the Kenyan coast should get in front of the specialists who sell Ethiopia's north, with heritage nights priced for the displaced week, not the peak. Safari camps with guests routing through Addis Ababa need do nothing yet but watch, and they should know what they are watching for.

Three indicators will tell the story. The first is any change to the British or American language on Addis Ababa itself, or on Bole. The second is any Ethiopian Airlines notice that reaches beyond the northern domestic points into its regional or long-haul network. The third is whether Obasanjo's mission produces direct talks, or whether the AU's warning about neighbours joining the escalation proves to have been the more accurate forecast.

The war in Ethiopia's north will not close East Africa's front door. It will test whether the region's hotels can tell their buyers, precisely and quickly, which doors are still open.
